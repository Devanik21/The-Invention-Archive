# When a Scientific AI Predicts Correctly but Cannot Know Why

A model can be right for the wrong reason. That statement is familiar in statistics, but it becomes especially important as AI systems are increasingly used to propose mechanisms, interpret high-dimensional measurements, and recommend scientific experiments. A predictor may reproduce an observed trajectory with impressive accuracy without establishing which process generated it. The distinction is not philosophical decoration. It determines whether the model can be trusted when a system is perturbed, transported to a new environment, or used to guide an intervention.

Recent work has explicitly warned that mechanistic learning from proxies can be underdetermined: several incompatible explanations may agree on the relationships visible in observational data. Earlier causal inference and experimental-design research has developed tools for resolving parts of this problem, including intervention selection and optimal experiment design. The contribution of this research note is a compact audit framework that asks a practical question: given a stated set of candidate mechanisms, what intervention evidence would be needed to distinguish them at a declared statistical error target?

The framework is called an *identifiability budget*. It is not a universal score for scientific truth. It is a costed design calculation under explicit assumptions, intended to expose when the available evidence is insufficient and to help choose informative experiments when a useful candidate set has already been defined.

## Prediction and identification are different targets

Suppose a system is observed under a narrow range of conditions. Two candidate models may match every measured point in that range. Their extrapolations can still disagree. If the observed distributions generated by the two mechanisms are exactly equal, no algorithm that only sees those observations can tell them apart better than its prior information allows. More samples from the same regime do not fix an absence of information. Larger neural networks do not fix it either.

This is the central observational-equivalence result. Let two candidate mechanisms be labelled M0 and M1. If both induce the same probability distribution for all observations collected under the existing design, then the likelihood ratio is one for every possible dataset. With equal prior odds, any decision rule based only on those observations is correct with probability one half. The conclusion holds regardless of model architecture or sample size.

That does not mean predictive AI is useless. If the task is to interpolate within a stable operating regime, an accurate predictor may be valuable even when its internal mechanism is unresolved. The problem arises when predictive accuracy is used as evidence for a unique scientific explanation, or when the model is carried into a regime where the competing hypotheses make different predictions.

## From disagreement to an experiment

The audit starts with a finite list of candidate mechanisms. This list may come from domain expertise, mechanistic equations, literature review, or AI-assisted hypothesis generation. The list must be treated as a working set rather than the full universe of possible explanations. A candidate generator can omit the true mechanism; a clean calculation cannot repair that omission.

Next, define a set of admissible experiments. An experiment might change a stimulus, initial condition, measurement time, chemical concentration, or operating input. Each candidate mechanism predicts an observation under each setting. If the predictions separate only in a condition that is unsafe, unethical, unavailable, or too expensive, the mathematical separation is not immediately useful. Practical feasibility belongs in the design objective from the beginning.

Under a simple but transparent statistical model, each experiment produces an independent Gaussian observation with known covariance that is the same under every candidate mechanism. If mechanism i predicts mean μᵢ and mechanism j predicts mean μⱼ, their separation is measured by a covariance-weighted squared distance. Summed over the planned experiments, this is the Kullback–Leibler divergence between the two candidate observation laws.

The divergence gives a direct measure of how much the proposed experiment can separate those two probability models under the assumptions. Zero means the candidates make identical mean predictions under all selected settings. A larger divergence means their predicted observation distributions are easier to distinguish. The statistical model matters: dependence between measurements, candidate-specific noise, or inaccurate predicted means can make a naive calculation overconfident.

## A finite-sample guarantee, with boundaries

For two Gaussian mechanisms with equal prior probabilities, known means, independent observations, and common covariance, the exact optimal pairwise classification error is Φ(−√(D/2)), where D is their cumulative Kullback–Leibler divergence and Φ is the standard normal cumulative distribution function. Thus, separation translates directly into an error probability.

For K candidate mechanisms, a union bound adds the pairwise error probabilities against the K−1 alternatives. Requiring each pairwise divergence to exceed a corresponding threshold is therefore a sufficient condition for a chosen maximum-likelihood model-selection error bound. It is conservative, and its scope is deliberately limited: it applies to the stated finite statistical model, not to every possible real-world mechanism.

This is where the identifiability budget enters. Assign each admissible intervention a cost, allow it to be repeated, and minimize total cost while meeting the pairwise information thresholds for the mechanism pairs that matter to the scientific decision. If no admissible plan separates a relevant pair, the budget is infinite. That result is not a failure of the optimizer. It is an explicit warning that the planned experiment set cannot resolve the requested decision under the specified model.

The distinction between a sufficient statistical condition and empirical scientific validation must remain visible. An achieved divergence threshold says the candidate distributions are separable *if the assumed response laws and noise model are correct*. It does not show that the list of mechanisms is complete, that the experiment manipulates the intended variable, or that the selected model describes nature. These are separate evidence obligations.

## A toy example where more observations do not help

Consider two response laws: M0 predicts f₀(u)=u, while M1 predicts f₁(u)=u². Observed measurements are collected only at u=0 and u=1, with Gaussian measurement noise of standard deviation one. The candidate means match exactly at both settings: zero at u=0 and one at u=1. No amount of repeated measurement at just these two settings can identify which candidate is correct, because both models produce the same observational distribution.

Now consider a new setting, u=2. The first model predicts two; the second predicts four. One independent observation at this setting contributes divergence D=2. With n repetitions, D=2n, and the exact equal-prior pairwise error becomes Φ(−√n). The analytic error is about 0.1587 for one replicate, 0.0787 for two, and 0.0416 for three. Under the stated assumptions, three replicates bring pairwise error below five per cent; two do not.

The example is intentionally small so that the logic can be verified without a complex solver. It is not a model of a biological mechanism and it is not evidence for an intervention in medicine. It demonstrates one general design principle: if candidate models coincide on the observed support, collect data where their predictions diverge, and quantify that divergence before committing the experiment budget.

## How AI fits without becoming its own evidence

AI can help generate candidate mechanisms from literature, fit model parameters, search a large intervention space, or approximate expensive forward simulations. These are useful roles, but each introduces uncertainty. The mechanism proposer may omit a rival. The forward model may be biased. An optimizer may exploit a region where the surrogate is least accurate. A fluent explanation may hide assumptions that should have been tested.

A disciplined workflow keeps these tasks separate. First, declare the scientific decision and the pairs of mechanisms that could change it. Second, record why each candidate is included and what alternatives were excluded. Third, check which mechanisms are observationally indistinguishable. Fourth, generate feasible interventions and estimate model-specific responses. Fifth, compute pairwise information under an explicit noise model. Sixth, optimize experiment cost and verify every required constraint. Finally, conduct the experiment, update the hypotheses, and report cases where the predicted separation failed.

Where AI supplies a surrogate model, the divergence calculation should be tested against high-fidelity simulations or pilot measurements. Where parameter estimates are uncertain, use uncertainty regions and worst-case separation rather than treating point predictions as known. Where samples are temporally or experimentally dependent, account for that dependence instead of summing independent information as though it were free. If a robust lower bound on separation is zero, the appropriate result may be abstention.

## What the audit does not solve

The method does not solve open-ended scientific discovery. The candidate set is finite, and the Gaussian assumptions are restrictive. Real mechanisms can contain unknown parameters, changing variance, batch effects, latent confounders, or observation channels that shift under intervention. The most informative mathematical intervention may also be impossible or ethically unacceptable. A model-selection guarantee within a misspecified candidate family can still select the wrong real-world explanation with high confidence.

These limitations point to the next evaluation step: a reproducible benchmark with models that are exactly equivalent on some training regimes, approximately equivalent under noise, and separable only under selected interventions. Baselines should include random intervention choice, largest raw prediction disagreement, local sensitivity-based design, and established active causal-design methods. The key measurements are not only predictive error, but also discrimination error, cost, sensitivity to model misspecification, calibration of simulated responses, and cases in which the system correctly refuses to choose.

The framework should be rejected or narrowed if it consistently recommends more expensive experiments than simple alternatives without better discrimination, if its budgets change radically under small plausible model perturbations, or if the assumptions needed for the guarantees cannot be checked in a practical setting. A failed audit can still be scientifically useful if it reveals that the candidate models are not distinguishable with the experiments currently available.

## The real standard is evidence, not eloquence

The broader point is simple: a scientific AI should be evaluated not only by how convincing its explanation sounds, but by whether its proposed mechanism makes predictions that could rule out competing explanations. When several mechanisms fit the observations, the system should preserve that ambiguity, select an informative experiment where possible, and state when no allowed experiment can settle the question.

The identifiability budget is a small step toward that standard. Its exact results hold only within the stated statistical model; its practical value remains to be evaluated on real scientific problems. But it makes a central obligation explicit: prediction, identification, and causal or physical validation are different claims, and they require different evidence.
