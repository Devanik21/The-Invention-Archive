    Abstract
     A cell-fate landscape is a useful summary, but it is not automatically a thermodynamic potential or a control-cost surface. This
paper tests a topology-aware workflow linking persistent homology, stochastic action, Hodge decomposition, Schrödinger bridges, and
reinforcement learning. In a one-dimensional quartic gradient model, the analytic barrier is 0.25 and the matched H-zero persistence
estimate is 0.249998, conditional on correct minimum–saddle pairing. Under observation noise, persistence calibration and pairing
degrade. An analytic circle example with first Betti number one shows that constant control can minimize effort: a matched-endpoint
pulse costs 3.33 times more, falsifying the unconditional claim that a loop forces pulsing. In 400 synthetic rollouts per policy, evenly
spaced pulses succeed in 23.25% of episodes, while the tabular RL policy achieves 7.75% and constant dosing 0% in the chosen regime.
RL therefore loses to a simple heuristic. All results are synthetic; no single-cell data were analyzed, no wet-lab experiments were run,
and no biological efficacy is claimed.
Keywords: cell-fate control; persistent homology; Freidlin–Wentzell action; Hodge decomposition; Schrödinger bridge; stochastic
optimal control; reinforcement learning; synthetic validation
Scope: synthetic mathematical benchmark only; not a validated cellular protocol.

1 Research question and contribution
Cell identity is not directly a point in a one-dimensional potential. It is a high-dimensional and partially observed
state shaped by transcriptional regulation, chromatin accessibility, signaling, metabolic context, cell-cycle state,
and stochastic molecular events. The landscape metaphor is useful because it makes stability, transition routes, and
competing attractors discussable in a common geometric language. It is also dangerous: a plotted scalar field may
be an inferred summary rather than a thermodynamic potential, and an attractive visual metaphor can be mistaken
for a quantitative law.
    This paper asks whether topological summaries can impose useful constraints on minimum-effort control
between cell-state distributions. The motivation is practical. Reprogramming protocols typically involve time-
varying transcription-factor expression, media changes, or other interventions whose sequence, amplitude, and
duration matter. Trial-and-error optimization is expensive. A data-driven controller might search a much larger
schedule space, but the optimization becomes fragile if the state representation is noisy, the dynamical model is
misidentified, or the objective ignores hidden paths.
    The project begins with three connected claims: (i) basin barriers are recoverable as zero-dimensional persis-
tence when a scalar gradient potential and the relevant minimum–saddle pairing are known; (ii) rotational drift and
topological cycles may make temporal schedules more useful than constant inputs; and (iii) reinforcement learning
(RL), regularized by stochastic path-control objectives, may discover schedules that exploit such structure. Each
claim has a different epistemic status, so we do not bundle them as a single theorem.
    The first part is a conditional mathematical identity, not a universal equivalence between persistence and
stochastic control cost. The second is a conjecture that can fail: nonzero first homology alone does not require
pulsed input. The third is an empirical question for a specified simulator. In our initial test, the RL policy does not
outperform an evenly spaced pulse heuristic. This negative result changes the paper’s central contribution from an
asserted topology-to-protocol law to an audit of where such a law would need additional assumptions.

1.1 Contributions and non-contributions
The paper provides four concrete outputs. First, it specifies the assumptions under which a potential barrier and an
H0 persistence value coincide. Second, it gives an analytic circle counterexample to an unconditional loop-implies-
pulsing statement. Third, it implements a deterministic, reproducible synthetic benchmark covering numerical
action convergence, persistence under noise, neural drift fitting, and matched-budget protocol comparisons. Fourth,
it records both favorable and unfavorable outputs and turns them into a falsifiable plan for eventual analysis of a
public reprogramming time course.
     It does not claim a new general theorem of cellular reprogramming; it does not claim that Hodge curl creates
a particular biological control barrier; it does not report learning from real single-cell data; it does not claim
RL superiority; and it does not propose a wet-lab dosing schedule. The principal methodological novelty claim
is modest: an integrated, fail-explicit workflow combining conditional landscape persistence, a Hodge-aware
dynamical interpretation, stochastic bridge objectives, and protocol benchmarks with explicit counterexamples. A
systematic priority search could still reveal closer integrated approaches.

2 Prior work and novelty boundary
2.1 Landscape models of cell fate
Classical developmental biology introduced the landscape metaphor as a way of visualizing constrained develop-
mental trajectories. Modern quantitative treatments derive or estimate a landscape from dynamical models and use
basin geometry to discuss stability and transitions. Work by Zhou and colleagues quantified landscape topography
and paths for differentiation, reprogramming, and transdifferentiation, reporting a relation between barrier heights
and transition rates in a specified gene-network model [2]. That history rules out a novelty claim for the broad
association between cell-fate landscape barriers and fate-transition difficulty.

    The relevant gap is narrower: an inferred scalar landscape and the actual stochastic control problem may differ
in metric, noise covariance, hidden variables, and non-gradient flux. A persistence interval is a property of a filtered
scalar function. A Freidlin–Wentzell quasipotential is a dynamical quantity defined relative to a drift and noise
covariance. They coincide in special cases, but neither object can silently substitute for the other. This distinction is
central to the present audit.

2.2 Persistent homology and uncertainty
Persistent homology records how connected components, loops, and higher-dimensional features appear and
disappear as a filtration threshold changes. The foundational theory and stability results give ways to compare
persistence diagrams under controlled perturbations [3, 4]. For a Morse potential, the elder-rule pairing in H0 can
associate a local minimum with the saddle level at which its component joins an older component. The interval
length is then a minimum–saddle height difference. This is not automatically the barrier from an arbitrary source
state to an arbitrary target basin: the pairing must describe that transition, and a scalar potential must exist.
    Data-derived landscapes add estimation error. Smoothing, feature selection, dimensionality reduction, and
sampling density affect critical points and their pairings. Persistence stability can control changes in the diagram
under a uniform perturbation of the scalar function, but it does not guarantee that the learned function is the correct
biological potential or that its minimum–saddle pairing corresponds to the relevant dynamic escape route. The
noise experiment in this paper demonstrates a particularly blunt failure mode: with increasing observation noise,
estimated persistence becomes biased upward and the target pairing is rarely identified by the simple grid-based
procedure.

2.3 Stochastic control and large deviations
For small-noise diffusions, large-deviation theory assigns an action to paths and gives exponential-scale predictions
for rare transitions. Kramers-type escape approximations relate transition rates to barrier structure under suitable
assumptions [5, 6]. The gradient-diffusion setting is especially interpretable: for an isotropic-noise SDE with drift
equal to minus the gradient of a potential, the quasipotential cost to cross a saddle agrees with the potential increase
in the usual noise normalization. In non-gradient systems, the quasipotential solves a dynamical variational problem
and generally cannot be reconstructed from a convenient scalar projection alone.

2.5 Research gap and positioning
We find substantive overlap in each ingredient and related efforts combining cell-fate inference, stochastic transport,
landscape estimation, and RL. The paper therefore does not claim the first use of topology, RL, or Schrödinger
bridges in cellular dynamics. The narrower question is whether a carefully separated set of topological and
dynamical diagnostics can predict when a schedule advantage is plausible and when that prediction should be
rejected. This work is a first reproducible stress test, not a validated universal solution. Search coverage included
primary literature, review and methods articles, DOI/PubMed/PMC records, the pbn-STAC code repository, and
GEO accessions. It was a focused search, not an exhaustive patent or all-databases priority review.

3.1 Assumptions that must be exposed
The simple barrier identity assumes a smooth enough scalar potential with a nondegenerate local minimum and
relevant index-one saddle, sublevel-set filtration of precisely that potential, and correct persistence pairing. The
simple FW interpretation further assumes an isotropic-noise gradient diffusion with a stated normalization and a
transition governed by the relevant saddle. The controller comparison assumes common initial-state distributions,
horizon, action budget, and success definition. The neural model assumes a specified state representation and
training sampling process. None of these assumptions is guaranteed by snapshot single-cell data.

4.2 Persistence pairing
Given the sublevel filtration V −1 ((−∞, a]), connected components are born at local minima and merge at saddle
levels. If a particular minimum is paired to the index-one saddle separating it from the older component according
to the elder rule, its H0 persistence is

                                                                 Pers0 (xm , xs ) = V (xs ) −V (xm ) = ∆V.                                                         (5)

Equation (5) is an equality of two height differences taken from the same scalar function. It is not a theorem that
a persistence interval computed from an arbitrary learned score equals the minimal control energy, a biological
energy, or the quasipotential barrier for non-gradient dynamics. For anisotropic noise, state-dependent diffusion,
non-gradient drift, or incorrect source-saddle pairing, the relevant quasipotential is defined by the controlled action
and may differ from raw sublevel persistence.

4.3 Numerical action check
The verification script discretizes candidate paths and evaluates the action integral at successively refined resolutions.
The absolute error declines from approximately 8.02 × 10−5 on the coarsest grid to 1.48 × 10−6 on the finest grid
in the saved convergence test. This is evidence that the implemented quadrature converges toward the analytic
result in this toy case. It is not an independent test of the gradient assumption or evidence that a real cell-state
transition follows that path.

5 Noisy landscapes and the stability gap
The stability theorem for persistence diagrams is valuable because a small uniform perturbation of a scalar function
constrains the bottleneck distance between diagrams. But the current use case introduces multiple uncertainties
at once: observed counts are noisy; a low-dimensional embedding may distort distances; the true drift may be
non-gradient; the potential may be misspecified; and the minimum-saddle pairing relevant to a transition can change
under perturbation. The theory does not erase these distinctions.
    We study one controlled perturbation: add Gaussian observation noise to sampled values of the synthetic quartic
potential, recompute the grid-based persistence, and assess whether the expected source–saddle feature is identified.
At zero noise, the median estimated persistence is 0.24994 and the target pairing is found in 100% of replicates.
At standard deviation 0.01, median persistence is 0.25910 and pairing rate 72.5%. At noise 0.03, the median is
0.28643 and pairing rate 35%; at noise 0.12, median persistence reaches 0.42190 and pairing rate is only 32.5%.
The median absolute error increases from about 5.95 × 10−5 without noise to 0.17190 at noise 0.12.
    This is a warning, not a universal estimate of the noise robustness of persistent homology. The simple grid
and pairing procedure is intentionally transparent but crude. A production method should use a stated smoothing
estimator, confidence sets for critical points, bootstrap persistence, sensitivity to embedding and metric, and
comparisons across plausible landscapes. The numerical trend cannot be explained away by a stability theorem
because the relevant function error, sampling scheme, and feature correspondence are not controlled as in the
theorem’s hypotheses.

5.1 Implication for control
A protocol optimizer should not treat a persistence estimate as a precisely known barrier. One defensible design is a
robust objective over an ensemble of fitted landscapes, with a penalty for policies that succeed only under a fragile
persistence pairing. Another is to require the relevant interval to be stable over a prespecified range of preprocessing
choices. Both choices trade performance for epistemic robustness and should be reported explicitly. The current
study does neither policy-level uncertainty propagation nor real-data bootstrapping, so that stage remains future
work.

6 Non-gradient drift and Hodge decomposition
A gradient system is only one special case. In a domain with an appropriate inner product and boundary conditions,
a vector field can be decomposed into gradient, co-exact/curl, and harmonic components. In a schematic Euclidean
notation,
                                          b(x) = −∇V (x) + c(x) + h(x),                                        (6)
where c is a rotational component orthogonal to gradients under the chosen Hodge inner product and h is a harmonic
component associated with global topology and boundary conditions. This schematic equation hides important
functional-analytic details: the decomposition is not unique until the domain, metric, boundary conditions, and
function spaces are specified. A discrete state-transition graph has its own edge-flow decomposition, which is not
automatically equivalent to a continuous gene-expression vector field.
    The central interpretive caution is that local circulation and global topology are different objects. A planar
vector field can have nonzero local curl even on a simply connected domain. A space with nontrivial H1 can
host loops, yet a specific endpoint-control problem on it can still have a constant optimal input. Thus the testable
hypothesis is not “curl creates pulses” but rather: under a stated control-affine dynamics, dose constraint, noise
model, endpoint requirement, and set of competing schedules, a measurable curl-related statistic predicts the
relative value of temporal variation beyond information already contained in drift, noise, and state representation.

7 Schrödinger bridges as a population-level objective
Single-cell sequencing generally samples different cells at different time points. A population snapshot provides an
empirical distribution, not a movie of the same cell. If µ0 and νT are source and target state distributions and P is a
reference path measure induced by an uncontrolled diffusion, the Schrödinger bridge selects a path measure Q that
minimizes relative entropy subject to endpoint marginals:

                                              Q⋆ =       arg min          DKL (Q∥P).                                          (9)
                                                     Q: Q0 =µ0 , QT =νT

Under compatible diffusion assumptions, this path-space KL objective corresponds to a stochastic control effort.
The exact conversion depends on diffusion and normalization; a model cannot use the phrase “minimum energy”
without specifying the reference process and control metric. Soft endpoint penalties, finite samples, and misspecified
noise change the optimization problem.
    For cell-state control the bridge is a useful complement to an individual-target hitting probability. It asks for a
low-relative-entropy evolution of an entire population while matching target marginals. This can discourage an
optimizer from sending only a small fraction of cells to the desired state while ignoring the rest. On the other hand,
a matching bridge between snapshots does not by itself identify cell lineages or establish a causal pathway. Multiple

8 Neural stochastic dynamics: what was fitted
A data-driven drift model can support control only if its predictive accuracy is evaluated on transitions not used for
fitting, noise is estimated separately, and errors in state regions important to the control policy are characterized.
We generated a synthetic stochastic dataset from the declared simulator, split 7,200 transitions for training and
1,800 for testing, then fitted an MLP regression model to predict drift. The test drift RMSE is 0.01169, and test R2
is 0.999927. The estimated diffusion standard deviation is 0.08146 against a synthetic truth of 0.08.
     These numbers are useful as software checks but are deliberately not framed as biological performance. The
train/test split is drawn from a common simulator rather than a held-out perturbation family, different cell line, donor,
or laboratory. A meaningful biological test would require independent perturbation settings and donors; calibration
of process versus measurement noise; uncertainty-aware prediction intervals; and comparison against simple
baselines such as linear drift, generalized additive models, or mechanistic gene-regulatory equations. Predictive
fit must be evaluated near the boundary of the target basin, since average RMSE can hide errors where control
decisions are most sensitive.

8.1 Minimal fit protocol for real data
For a future single-cell application, the analysis should start from an explicitly documented processed matrix and
sample metadata, preserve donor and batch groupings, and pre-register the state representation. It should avoid
leakage from future time points into drift training. Model choice should be compared with linear and mechanistic
baselines. Residual diffusion should not be assumed isotropic unless diagnostics support that simplification. The fit
should be accompanied by posterior or bootstrap ensembles, calibration metrics, out-of-distribution tests, and an
explicit stop rule if the predicted drift is not identifiable from cross-sectional measurements.

10 Dose matching and schedule sensitivity
Equal nominal dose is an important but incomplete control. The constant schedule uses a low level at every step. A
pulse schedule can use high amplitude for fewer steps, and the same L1 integral does not mean the same internal
state exposure when the system is nonlinear. Nor does it control the time derivative, frequency content, duration
above threshold, or toxicity. We therefore define the dose budget explicitly, keep the horizon fixed, and report the
amplitude bound used by the pulse baseline. This is a fairer comparison than comparing protocols with unequal
total administered input, but it is not a complete experimental equivalence.
    The sensitivity grid spans five values each for the adaptation parameter and noise scale, with 40 trials per policy
and cell (50 cells in total). When the adaptation parameter is zero, both simple policies succeed nearly always at
the lowest-noise settings. For adaptation parameter 6—the setting of the headline benchmark—the evenly spaced
pulses are stronger than constant dosing across the reported noise grid; by noise standard deviation 0.12, their
success frequency is 0.35 versus 0 for constant dosing. The result changes with parameter settings, so no general
claim that temporal variation always wins is warranted.
    The increase in success frequency with noise at high adaptation is a model-dependent stochastic-resonance-like
pattern, not a validated biological claim. It should be compared against alternative reward definitions and trajectory
metrics. A basic check is whether apparent success is driven by broad noise-induced target crossing rather than
control. That comparison is not complete here and is an explicit limitation.

11 Evaluation protocol and baselines
We separate three layers of evidence: (i) analytic identities and counterexamples, (ii) synthetic model tests, and
(iii) real-data and ultimately experimental tests. In the current paper only the first two have results. The public
single-cell resource is identified for the future third layer, but its matrix is not downloaded or analyzed in the current
cycle. This separation is central to the interpretation of every metric.
     A robust evaluation should include: a gradient potential with known barrier; a non-gradient system with a known
quasipotential or numerical action solution; a circular or graph-structured state space; perturbation experiments
over data noise; multiple control budgets; a policy test with matched action constraints; and a counterexample to
each proposed implication. We implemented only a subset of this matrix. The completed analytic tests include the
quartic landscape and circle endpoint problem; the simulation covers noisy persistence, synthetic drift fitting, and
one policy regime plus a sensitivity grid. Full multi-attractor networks, uncertainty propagation from neural drift
into policies, and a genuine Schrödinger bridge solver are not implemented.

12 Falsifiable predictions and decisive negative tests
The paper proposes a restrained set of predictions that would make a future study scientifically useful even if the
framework fails. They are scoped to models and datasets, not phrased as clinical or wet-lab instructions.

12.1 Prediction A: barrier–persistence agreement has a conditional domain
In systems known to be close to gradient diffusions, and when the source minimum is correctly paired with the
saddle separating it from the target basin, bootstrap persistence estimates should track an independently estimated
quasipotential barrier within a tolerance that accounts for potential-fit and discretization error. The prediction is
falsified if the relationship fails after controlling state representation, metric, sample size, and model uncertainty—or
if a non-gradient alternative consistently predicts transition rates better from dynamical action than from persistence.
This test must compare at least one non-gradient system, not only repeat the same quartic toy example.

12.2 Prediction B: schedule benefit requires dynamic evidence beyond Betti number
A cycle-related measure should predict out-of-sample benefit from temporal variation only after controlling for
state-space dimension, local drift magnitude, controllability Gramian, target distance, and noise covariance. The
circle counterexample already falsifies the unconditional version. A test that uses b1 alone as a predictor should be
considered failed if it does not outperform random topology-label permutations or if local curl explains the result
equally well in simply connected domains.

12.3 Prediction C: RL must generalize beyond the training simulator
If RL provides value, its learned policy must improve over equally budgeted pulse, greedy, model-predictive, and
dynamic-programming baselines on held-out parameter regimes, random seeds, and model classes. Reporting only
training returns would be insufficient. A fair comparison freezes policy hyperparameters before test evaluation,
uses confidence intervals, and reports all attempted seeds. If the RL policy’s mean performance is lower than a
simple heuristic across the prespecified test grid, the RL-superiority claim is rejected even if one tuned setting
shows a win.

12.4 Prediction D: uncertainty-aware topology helps, or it should be dropped
Landscape uncertainty is useful only if carrying an ensemble through policy optimization improves calibration or
robustness against model misspecification. If it adds computational complexity without improving held-out success
or reducing worst-case failure, it should not remain in the objective merely to preserve theoretical elegance.

13 Failure modes, robustness, and uncertainty
The most immediate issue is landscape non-identifiability. A scalar potential inferred from stationary density
assumes a relationship between drift and noise. In a non-equilibrium steady state, probability currents can exist,
and the stationary density alone does not identify those currents. A learned energy-like scalar can fit marginal
density while failing to predict transition paths. Consequently, a persistence score should be treated as a feature of
the estimation pipeline—not as a directly observed molecular energy.
     Second, graph topology depends on how the data are transformed. Principal-component projections, diffusion
maps, and latent models can merge distinct cell states or introduce apparent loops. Feature filtration and nearest-
neighbor parameters also change the persistence diagram. Stability of the persistence diagram under perturbations
to a fixed scalar function is not a substitute for uncertainty over the embedding and the learned drift. In the synthetic
noise test, pairing rates deteriorate much faster than one might infer from an eyeballed smooth landscape.

    Third, the controller’s objective may be a poor proxy for actual success. Endpoint-only reward can ignore

informed by experimental teams, not a reward function optimized in isolation.
    Fourth, RL can exploit simulator artifacts, reward shaping, and distribution shifts. The present RL policy did
not beat the evenly spaced pulse policy, which is precisely why both should remain in the evidence record. More
iterations do not automatically fix model misspecification. A policy that wins only when the transition model is
exact can fail when the biological system differs from the training simulator. Robust evaluation must vary drift
parameters, diffusion, response delay, and action nonlinearities before a policy earns any real-data claim.

15 Synthetic network models and model-class extension
Toggle-switch networks offer a compact way to create multiple attractors: mutually repressive regulators can
support alternative stable expression states, depending on parameters and molecular noise. Ring-like repressilator
networks illustrate oscillatory dynamics rather than a scalar basin picture. These architectures are familiar examples,
not newly proposed biological mechanisms. They are useful because they expose the difference between basin
topology, local rotational dynamics, and temporal protocol response.
    The current scripts draw network schematics as conceptual diagrams and do not simulate those network graphs
as the policy benchmark. The paper therefore does not claim that the controller result generalizes from the scalar
bistable SDE to a toggle switch or repressilator. A credible extension would implement known synthetic regulatory
equations, compare deterministic attractors with stochastic transitions, calculate graph- or state-space topology
from sampled trajectories, and test whether a topology-derived metric adds predictive value beyond drift and
controllability features. It would also compare model-generated observations with the true simulator state to
quantify the effect of measurement noise.

15.1 A minimum suite of synthetic ground truths
We propose four escalating classes. Class I is the quartic one-dimensional gradient potential, used for analytical
calibration. Class II is a non-gradient rotor or planar system with known circulation, used to test the cycle hypothesis.
Class III is a multi-attractor regulatory network with known governing parameters, used to distinguish true basins
from projection artifacts. Class IV is a partially observed stochastic system with unknown parameters, used for
model-identification stress tests. A success in Class I is necessary for implementation verification but nowhere near
sufficient for biological utility.

16 Control-theoretic conditions for schedules
The optimal control law depends not only on topology but on the control channel B(x), the endpoint set, time
horizon, amplitude limits, state-dependent drift, noise covariance, and objective. If the system is linear with
quadratic cost and unconstrained endpoints, the optimal controller may be smooth and continuous. If input is
bounded, response delay matters, or the system has a threshold and strongly nonlinear drift, bang-bang or pulsed
controls may arise. Those features are control-theoretic, not consequences of the first Betti number by itself.

17 Discussion: what the evidence allows us to say
The conceptual appeal of connecting persistent homology, Hodge decomposition, stochastic bridges, and RL is
that each describes a different aspect of the problem: connectivity across energy thresholds, rotational versus
potential-like flow, population-level path distributions, and sequential policy search. The difficulty is exactly that
these quantities live at different mathematical levels. Joining them produces an explanatory hypothesis, not a result.
A strong paper has to test whether the connection adds information instead of simply placing the terms beside one
another.
     The quartic potential demonstrates a real but narrow identity: with a valid scalar potential and correct pairing,
H0 persistence equals the barrier height. The noise sweep demonstrates fragility of the simplest estimator under
observation perturbation. The circle example decisively rules out the broad loop-implies-pulsing claim. The
controller benchmark adds a second negative result: under this model and reward, RL fails to beat a straightforward
pulse heuristic. Together, these findings argue for a pipeline that uses topology as an uncertain diagnostic, retains
explicit dynamical information, and keeps simple baselines central.
     The remaining research opportunity is still meaningful. A carefully identified Hodge-like feature might
complement controllability and path action when predicting schedule benefit. A bridge objective may help match
full populations better than endpoint hit-rate optimization. And RL might be valuable in a richer environment
where policies need to adapt to state estimates and uncertain response. Each claim should remain a hypothesis until
it beats alternatives in held-out models and eventually in suitable experimental datasets.

17.1 Human benefit and translation boundary
If future work identifies a robust relationship between state-space structure and intervention schedule, it could help
experimentalists prioritize candidate reprogramming schedules and reduce uninformative trial-and-error. The causal
pathway is indirect: reliable state inference would inform an in-silico schedule, which would then need independent
experimental testing and safety assessment. The present work is far from those stages. It cannot specify doses,
predict clinical efficacy, guarantee cell quality, or justify a protocol in human cells. There are significant barriers in
measurement noise, batch and donor heterogeneity, biological safety, off-target states, ethics, and reproducibility.

18 Limitations
The principal limitation is scope of validation. All numerical outputs are synthetic and driven by known model
equations. No public single-cell expression data have been downloaded or fitted. No wet-lab experiments,
cellular assays, or clinical outcomes are included. Consequently, the manuscript does not show that the proposed
combination works on real cellular reprogramming.
    Second, the neural drift estimator is trained and tested on simulated transitions generated from the same
model family. Its excellent test score is an implementation benchmark under favorable assumptions. It does not
demonstrate the identifiability of drift from sparse single-cell snapshots, nor does it model bursty transcription,
nonstationary noise, donor effects, or measurement artifacts.
    Third, the topology code uses a discretized one-dimensional potential and a simple pairing procedure. While
adequate for a known quartic function, it does not solve the challenge of high-dimensional persistent homology on
latent embeddings. The robustness experiment adds noise to a one-dimensional function and reveals poor feature
correspondence; it is illustrative, not a calibrated error model for RNA sequencing.
    Fourth, the Hodge decomposition is conceptual, and the scripts do not estimate a Hodge field from observed
trajectories. Neither the Hodge curl nor first homology is connected empirically to the chosen control simulator.
The circle example is a mathematical counterexample, not a cellular model.
    Fifth, the bridge optimization is formulated but no numerical Schrödinger bridge is solved. The RL imple-
mentation uses a tabular agent and a scalar state. The finite policy benchmark has no confidence intervals from
independent training seeds, and the policy was not tuned against all strong baselines. These limitations narrow the
strength of the conclusions but do not justify hiding the negative outcome.
    Finally, the literature search was focused and current as of 24 October 2026 but not exhaustive across every
database, patent collection, or unpublished result. Novelty is therefore deliberately stated as an integration and
stress-test contribution, not a priority claim.

19 Conclusion
Topological summaries can constrain interpretations of a scalar landscape, but their relationship to control cost
is conditional. In a gradient diffusion with a correctly paired source minimum and saddle, barrier height and
H0 persistence are the same height difference. In noisy inferred landscapes, the correct pairing and calibration
can deteriorate. In non-gradient settings, a scalar persistence value need not equal the quasipotential barrier. A
nontrivial loop does not require a pulsed optimum, as the analytic circle counterexample shows. In the synthetic
controller benchmark, RL does not beat evenly spaced pulses, and the paper reports that failure rather than claiming
success.
    The defensible next step is a preregistered, uncertainty-aware comparison across synthetic ground-truth networks
followed—only if the synthetic gates survive—by a reproducible analysis of public time-course data. The project’s
useful hypothesis is not that topology dictates a protocol, but that topological features may become informative
control features under explicit, testable dynamical assumptions. Until those assumptions and out-of-sample
advantages are demonstrated, topology, Hodge structure, bridges, and RL remain complementary tools, not a
proven recipe for cellular reprogramming.

C Synthetic model and benchmark specification
The release code is the authoritative executable specification for simulator parameters, random seeds, action bounds,
reward function, rollout logic, training settings, and plot generation. The model is intentionally small enough to run
deterministically without downloading any data. The potential is V (x) = (x2 − 1)2 /4. A drift estimator is trained
on 7,200 synthetic transitions and tested on 1,800 transitions; the policy study uses 400 evaluation rollouts for each
of four controllers. Sensitivity results contain 50 parameter–noise–policy cells with 40 trials per cell. The random
seed for the main verification is 20261024; the neural drift training subroutine uses seed 20261035 as recorded in
the machine-readable report.
    The verifier checks the analytic barrier, persistence, action convergence, neural-fit metrics, dose bounds, circle
counterexample, policy outputs, sensitivity grid, replicate retention, and figure generation. Passing certifies code
checks only; CSV outputs permit recomputation, and RL superiority over pulses is not asserted.

D Minimal public-data analysis protocol
A public-data follow-up should preserve the following order: (1) verify GSE242423/GSE242424 metadata and
access conditions; (2) acquire available processed matrices and checksums; (3) predefine QC, batch handling,
and a reduced state representation; (4) split evaluation by time, donor, or experiment to avoid leakage; (5) fit
multiple drift/noise models and quantify uncertainty; (6) estimate persistent features across bootstrap samples
and embeddings; (7) solve a bridge or transport baseline with documented assumptions; (8) compare constant,
simple pulse, model-predictive, dynamic-programming, and RL policies on simulated counterfactuals only where
identified; (9) report non-identifiability and negative results; and (10) submit protocol hypotheses for experimental
assessment without describing simulated schedules as validated interventions.
    The analysis should be stopped if state-space inference or noise estimation is not sufficiently identifiable. The
current release has not run any of these data-acquisition and empirical-modeling steps. This appendix is a protocol
for a future computational phase, not a claim that the phase has been completed.

E Reproducibility and interpretation
To reproduce the synthetic checks, install the Python dependencies listed in results/environment.json and
execute:

python verify_model.py

The script regenerates output tables and figure assets. Running the script changes generated outputs if a dependency
implementation or plotting backend differs; record exact package versions and compare CSV values before
interpreting differences. Use a clean working directory as a test of path independence. The PDF build command is
documented in the release README and deployment script.
    The release deliberately distinguishes three meanings of “validation”: program assertions, agreement with an
analytical toy result, and empirical biological confirmation. This work achieves the first two for the named tests
and achieves none of the third. The public dataset accession is a planned candidate only. No experiment, cell line,
sample, dose, patient, or biological treatment was studied here.
