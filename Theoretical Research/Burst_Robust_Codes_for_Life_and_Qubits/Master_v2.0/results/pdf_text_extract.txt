Scope boundary
This manuscript distinguishes a shared mathematical vocabulary from shared physical mecha-
nisms. Radiation-induced events can generate correlated disturbances in superconducting-qubit
devices; high-LET ion tracks can generate clustered DNA lesions in cells. The two systems do
not have the same state space, microscopic dynamics, recovery mechanism, or failure observable.
Holographic quantum error correction is not a model of DNA repair. The numerical experi-
ment in this paper is a deliberately limited lattice-connectivity surrogate, not a quantum-code
decoder, a radiation-transport calculation, or a biological experiment.


                                              Abstract
      Average disturbance rates can conceal consequential spatial and temporal structure when faults
  arrive in bursts and recovery resources are ﬁnite. Radiation-associated correlated errors have been
  observed in superconducting-qubit systems, while high-linear-energy-transfer particles can create
  clustered DNA lesions. These domains have diﬀerent microscopic mechanisms; this paper develops
  only a shared statistical language for marked events, spatial footprints, and constrained recovery.
  Each event carries location, orientation, extent, severity, temporal response, and a workload mark.
  Separate substrate maps translate these marks into quantum error channels or biological lesion
  ﬁelds, followed by code-speciﬁc decoding or biological repair.
      We state conditional results for nonlinear failure under correlated occupancy, stability of a
  stationary M/G/1 workload model, and Poisson thinning of catastrophic marked events. We then
  compare independent erasures with Poisson ﬁnite-track bursts in a reproducible 24 by 24 lattice
  surrogate. Track intensity is calibrated by bisection so expected lattice-wide mean occupancy
  matches the independent-erasure probability; sitewise marginals remain heterogeneous near clipped
  boundaries. Across 4,000 trials at each of ten probabilities, this particular track process has a
  higher spanning frequency at every tested point. At target occupancy 0.40, it produces 705/4,000
  spanning events versus 0/4,000 for independent erasures; zero observed events do not imply zero
  risk.
      The contribution is a testable decomposition of event statistics, substrate damage, recovery,
  and reliability inference, plus a falsiﬁable validation programme. The benchmark is not a physical
  decoder, radiation-transport calculation, or biological experiment. No universal quantum threshold,
  cellular repair limit, or intervention is claimed. The neutrino fog is treated only as an inference
  analogy from dark-matter detection.

1    Introduction: reliability under spatially extended events
The reliability of a complex system is not determined solely by how often its components are disturbed.
The geometry of a disturbance, its duration, the dependence it induces among components, and
the ability of the system to observe and recover from it all matter. This point is familiar in fault-
tolerant computing: independent low-probability faults can be manageable under an appropriate
code and decoder, whereas correlated faults may form a geometrically dangerous pattern. It is also
familiar in radiobiology: two irradiations delivering similar macroscopic dose can produce diﬀerent
microscopic lesion structures, and the structure and complexity of DNA damage aﬀect repair and
biological outcomes [Dennis et al., 2002, Fowler et al., 2012, Hagiwara et al., 2019, Goodhead and
Weinfeld, 2024, Wilkinson et al., 2023].
   Recent measurements make this distinction timely for quantum hardware. Castelli and colleagues
reported correlations between detected radiation events and changes in superconducting-qubit relax-
ation and phase-coherence times, including reductions of up to 30.5% immediately following dual
detector events attributed to penetrating muons [Castelli et al., 2026]. Binney and colleagues distin-
guished radiation-associated correlated errors from vibration-associated errors using temporal, spatial,
and frequency-domain signatures in superconducting-qubit devices [Binney et al., 2026]. These results
motivate event-conditioned noise models. They do not imply that an individual muon invariably causes
a logical failure, nor that every event is catastrophic. Their importance is that the disturbance process
can have structure omitted by an IID approximation.
   A parallel statistical issue appears in the response of cells to high-LET heavy ions. Dense energy
deposition along particle tracks can produce complex and clustered DNA lesions, including clustered
double-strand breaks, and can trigger repair pathways whose outcomes depend on lesion context and
chromatin organization [Hagiwara et al., 2019, Shibata, 2022, Goodhead and Weinfeld, 2024]. A 2026
track-structure simulation database spanning H–Fe ions provides a concrete example of eﬀorts to
characterize radiation-quality-speciﬁc damage rather than represent all exposure through one scalar
average [Huang et al., 2026]. Again, this does not imply that every track overwhelms repair. It
motivates a model in which event geometry and repair workload remain explicit.
   The present study asks a narrower question than a uniﬁed theory of quantum hardware and biology:
What mathematical objects are shared by systems exposed to marked spatial bursts and ﬁnite recovery
capacity, and which inferences remain domain-speciﬁc? The answer is a layered model. A marked
event process generates spatial and temporal footprints. A substrate map translates those footprints
into error channels or lesion ﬁelds. A recovery map then describes decoding or repair. Finally, an
observable-speciﬁc loss function deﬁnes failure. The layers are coupled, but not interchangeable.
   The term “codes for life” in the title is therefore an analogy about recoverability under constrained
resources, not a claim that a cell implements a quantum error-correcting code. A cell has DNA repair
pathways, chromatin organization, checkpoints, cell-cycle state, and survival constraints; a quantum
processor has a code space, stabilizer measurements, a decoder, and logical observables. Similar
probability and workload tools may describe selected aspects of both, while the actual state variables
and causal mechanisms remain diﬀerent.
   This paper makes six contributions. First, it formalizes a marked space-time representation of
disturbance events with geometry and severity marks. Second, it separates event statistics from
substrate-speciﬁc damage and from recovery capacity. Third, it states queue-stability and Poisson-
thinning results with explicit assumptions. Fourth, it proves by a minimal counterexample that
equal marginal occupancy does not determine nonlinear failure risk. Fifth, it expands the original
ﬁnite-lattice experiment with exact expected-occupancy calibration, uncertainty summaries, spatial-
correlation and cluster diagnostics, ﬁnite-size sensitivity, and track-length sensitivity. Sixth, it speciﬁes
quantum and biological experiments that could falsify the proposed model components.

2     Epistemic contract and scope of inference
A long paper can be misleading if the visual scale of the document exceeds the evidential scale of
its results. We therefore begin with a scope contract. Statements are divided into four categories:
published observations, mathematical consequences of stated assumptions, synthetic outputs from the
included code, and proposed experiments. These categories are never merged into a single evidence
grade.


2.1   Published observations

The quantum-hardware literature establishes that radiation-associated quasiparticle and phonon pro-
cesses can correlate with qubit errors and coherence changes. Castelli et al. provide detector-
synchronized measurements of qubit parameters, while Binney et al. provide a comparative account
of radiation and vibration error signatures [Castelli et al., 2026, Binney et al., 2026]. These are device-
speciﬁc measurements. They are evidence for structured disturbance channels, not universal material
constants and not a measured catastrophic-event probability for a full fault-tolerant computer.
   The radiobiology literature establishes that high-LET radiation can generate complex, clustered
DNA damage, and that lesion architecture can inﬂuence repair kinetics and chromosome-level out-
comes [Hagiwara et al., 2019, Shibata, 2022, Goodhead and Weinfeld, 2024, Wilkinson et al., 2023].
The 2026 H–Fe simulation database strengthens the availability of species- and energy-dependent
track-structure outputs, but a simulation database does not eliminate the need for model validation
and uncertainty accounting [Huang et al., 2026].

2.3      Synthetic outputs

The numerical results in this paper come from an idealized ﬁnite lattice. Each “track” is a ﬁnite

whether an erased component spans the lattice under four-neighbour connectivity. No charge trans-
port, phonon propagation, syndrome measurement, stabilizer decoding, DNA chemistry, chromatin
state, lesion repair enzyme, or cell-fate dynamics is simulated. The benchmark is useful because the
dependence structure is transparent and reproducible; its limitations are equally central to the paper.


2.4      Proposed work

Calibration from radiation detectors, Geant4 or PARTRAC track structure, quantum hardware, repair
microscopy, or biological assays is a future programme. Neural decoders may be trained or adapted to
measured event-conditioned noise, but no published decoder is claimed to have already been validated
on the exact burst law used here. AlphaQubit 2 is a recent demonstration of scalable real-time neural
decoding for topological codes under its evaluated settings; transfer to the radiation-conditioned
channel is an open experiment [Senior et al., 2025].

    Scope boundary
    No result in the lattice benchmark is called a surface-code threshold, a holographic-code thresh-
    old, a DNA damage threshold, a mutation probability, a lifespan estimate, or a biological repair
    limit. No claim is made that “one muon defeats a quantum computer.” No claim is made that
    solar neutrinos meaningfully damage cellular DNA.



3     Quantum reliability: radiation, correlated errors, and decoding
3.1      From radiation events to qubit disturbances

Ionizing radiation can create energetic phonons and quasiparticles in superconducting substrates and
circuit components. Those excitations can propagate and interact with multiple qubits, generating
time-correlated error opportunities. The operational measurement is not simply an incoming particle
count: it can include detector event times, detector-array coincidence patterns, qubit relaxation times,
dephasing metrics, quasiparticle-sensitive observables, and the spatial response across a device. These


                                                     5
BURST-ROBUST CODES                                                             PREPRINT • 06 NOV 2026



signals live at diﬀerent levels of the causal chain and should not be substituted for one another.
   Castelli et al. synchronize microwave kinetic-inductance detector arrays with transmon measure-
ments and report statistically signiﬁcant changes in T1 and T2 after selected detected events [Castelli
et al., 2026]. The result supports the use of event-conditioned covariates. A disciplined statistical
analysis would preserve the detector time stamp, coincidence multiplicity, event geometry, measure-
ment latency, device conﬁguration, and competing explanations. The measured percentage change in
a coherence parameter is not a logical error rate: a logical rate additionally depends on the code, the
decoder, measurement schedule, correlations across cycles, and how often the event occurs.
   Binney et al. demonstrate why classiﬁcation of the disturbance source matters. Radiation-related
signatures and pulse-tube vibration signatures can both produce correlated errors, yet have diﬀerent
temporal, spatial, and spectral ﬁngerprints [Binney et al., 2026]. A model that labels all correlations
as radiation-induced would confound distinct physical processes. The event mark should therefore
include source class or a posterior distribution over source classes, and the inference should retain
uncertainty in that assignment.


3.2   From physical faults to a quantum channel
           ⊗
Let HP = ni=1 Hi denote the physical-qubit Hilbert space and let C ⊂ HP be the code subspace. A
disturbance event z induces a quantum channel Nz or, in a simpliﬁed Pauli description, a distribution
over error operators E. A conventional IID model might assign independent error indicators Xi ∼
Bernoulli(p). A correlated event model instead conditions local error probabilities on a shared random
event:

       P(Xi = 1 | Z = z) = ri (z),      Cov(Xi , Xj ) = E[Cov(Xi , Xj | Z)] + Cov(ri (Z), rj (Z)).     (1)

The second covariance term can remain positive even when conditional faults are independent given
the event. A shared radiation burst is one possible common cause. This construction is not limited
to radiation; vibration, control leakage, thermal excursions, and correlated calibration failures can
produce analogous dependence structures.
   For a code with distance d, the familiar statement that arbitrary t = ⌊(d − 1)/2⌋ errors are cor-
rectable is a worst-case combinatorial guarantee, not a prediction for a particular stochastic channel.
Under a stochastic channel, logical performance depends on the distribution of error patterns and
decoder. Under an erasure channel, the location of each erasure is known to the decoder, and cor-
rectability is governed by whether the erased set supports an undetectable logical operator. These
are diﬀerent channel assumptions. Radiation-associated disturbance should not automatically be rep-
resented as a known erasure: it may be a mixture of dephasing, relaxation, leakage, measurement
faults, and burst-induced correlations.


3.3   Why correlations matter to decoders

A decoder that assumes factorized likelihoods may score a correlated event as many independent
faults. The same total number of single-qubit error indicators can produce radically diﬀerent geom-
etry depending on where those errors land. If a cluster aligns with a logical operator or creates an
ambiguous syndrome history, a code may fail at a rate not predicted by mean occupancy alone. Con-
versely, a decoder that knows the event-conditioned likelihood may exploit the structure and improve
performance, provided the event model is calibrated and the decoder can process the information
within the cycle deadline.
   A relevant operational metric is a logical failure probability per round, pL (d, η, τ ), conditioned on
code distance d, channel parameters η, and decoder latency budget τ . Another is the probability
that a radiation event generates a correlated fault pattern outside a decoder’s modeled support. The

same code, the same decoder, identical measurement schedule, equal latency constraints, and held-out
event traces. If event detection is used as side information, the detector latency and false-positive/false-

3.4   Holographic quantum error correction: a code-theoretic lens

Holographic codes provide a particularly useful example of geometry-dependent recoverability. Tensor-
network constructions encode bulk logical degrees of freedom into boundary degrees of freedom, and
can represent reconstruction of a logical operator on diﬀerent boundary regions. The original HaPPY
construction makes this relation explicit in a family of toy models [Pastawski et al., 2015]. Harlow
showed how quantum error-correction language explains a version of the Ryu–Takayanagi relation
and entanglement-wedge reconstruction [Harlow, 2017]. Later CSS holographic codes and threshold
analyses have developed the code family and its erasure properties [Harris et al., 2018, Bao et al.,
2022].
    For a speciﬁed encoding isometry V : HL → HP and erased boundary subset E, exact erasure
correction of a logical subsystem can be expressed through the Knill–Laﬂamme condition. If P = V V †
is the code projector and {Ea } spans the error set supported on E, then

                                      P Ea† Eb P = cab P   for all a, b.                                (2)

For erasures, the locations are known and the condition can be recast through decoupling of the erased
region from the logical subsystem. In holographic settings, reconstruction regions and entanglement
wedges depend on the code geometry and selected logical algebra. The existence of a holographic
code does not confer a universal tolerance to arbitrary extended bursts. A burst must be mapped
onto the code’s physical boundary degrees of freedom and tested against the code-speciﬁc erasure or
general-noise correctability condition.
   A physically honest project would instantiate at least one explicit code family, publish its tensors or
stabilizers, deﬁne the boundary placement of the physical qubits, construct burst-conditioned channels,
run the decoder, and measure logical failure and decoding time over code sizes. The lattice experiment
in this manuscript performs none of those code-speciﬁc steps. It oﬀers a geometric warning that an
equal-occupancy comparison can conceal major diﬀerences in cluster structure; it is not a holographic
threshold calculation.


4     Radiobiology: high-LET tracks and clustered lesions
4.1   Track structure is more informative than dose alone

Absorbed dose is an energy-per-mass summary. It is an essential exposure quantity, but it does not
uniquely specify the nanoscale or microscale spatial organization of energy deposition. High-LET
charged particles deposit energy densely along tracks. Depending on species, energy, geometry, cell
state, and chromatin context, the resulting damage can include single-strand breaks, double-strand
breaks, base damage, chemically complex lesions, and clusters of lesions separated by short distances
[Hagiwara et al., 2019, Shibata, 2022, Goodhead and Weinfeld, 2024, Wilkinson et al., 2023].
   The scale of lesion complexity matters. A locally dense cluster may require coordinated processing
and can pose diﬀerent risks than the same count of isolated lesions. But a lesion cluster is not
equivalent to an unrepaired break, and an unrepaired break is not equivalent to mutation, chromosome
aberration, or cell death. Each endpoint has a separate measurement deﬁnition and time scale. A
model that collapses all endpoints into a single binary “cell failed” label discards the causal sequence
that experiments are intended to resolve.

response variables. For example, a radial dose kernel is not itself a double-strand-break probability;

5.1   Marked space-time events

Let (Ω, F, P) be a probability space. A disturbance history is represented by a marked point process

Here Tk is event time, Xk is an anchor location, Θk describes orientation or morphology, Lk is an
extent or scale parameter, Wk is severity or workload, and Mk is a source or mechanism mark. The
location space may be a device graph, a two-dimensional chip layout, a three-dimensional cell nucleus,
or a genomic coordinate system. Time may be continuous, discrete, or a mixed event/cycle index.

or a physically speciﬁed transport-derived process. A homogeneous Poisson process is an analytically
convenient baseline, not a claim that cosmic-ray events or DNA lesions are universally Poisson in every
observed coordinate. Event clustering can arise from parent-child processes, latent environmental
ﬂuctuations, or a common physical event that generates a multi-site footprint. The model class
should be selected from detector and assay data rather than from mathematical convenience alone
[Last and Penrose, 2017, Moller and Waagepetersen, 2004].

5.4   Observable-speciﬁc failure

Let YQ denote a quantum logical observable, such as a logical Pauli error in one correction cycle or
a logical failure during a ﬁxed algorithmic horizon. Let YB denote a biological endpoint, such as
persistent clustered lesions at time τ , chromosome aberration frequency, mutation burden, clonogenic
survival, or a deﬁned functional phenotype. A generic reliability event is

                                 FQ = {YQ ∈ FQ },           FB = {YB ∈ FB }.                                    (8)

The failure sets are not interchangeable. A comparison is meaningful only at the level of shared
statistical descriptors, not by equating FQ and FB .


6     Recovery as a ﬁnite-capacity process
6.1   Quantum recovery is not a single-server queue

A quantum decoder consumes measurement outcomes and produces corrections or Pauli-frame up-
dates. Its workload can include syndrome processing, belief updates, neural inference, communication,
and memory movement. The cycle deadline is a real-time constraint: even an accurate correction may
be operationally too late if it arrives after the control system requires it. Nevertheless, quantum re-
covery is not generally reducible to one scalar repair server. A queue is at most a useful abstraction
for a chosen processing subsystem, such as a decoder pipeline or a ﬁnite bandwidth path for event
metadata.


                                                       10
BURST-ROBUST CODES                                                           PREPRINT • 06 NOV 2026



   For a code, recoverability is ﬁrst a channel-and-code property. The decoder then approximates
or implements an optimal or near-optimal inference rule under the supplied noise model. The per-
formance metrics include logical failure probability, decoding latency, throughput, memory use, and
degradation under distribution shift. A decoder trained on independent depolarizing noise may be
miscalibrated under rare correlated bursts even if the marginal single-qubit error rate is unchanged.


6.2   Biological repair workload

DNA repair is distributed across multiple molecular pathways and regulated by cell state. A lesion
may be recognized, processed, resected, ligated, bypassed, or left persistent; pathway use can depend
on lesion chemistry, cell-cycle phase, chromatin accessibility, and local protein availability. A scalar
service capacity µ therefore cannot represent the whole biological response. It can be a coarse aggre-
gate for a restricted assay or a theoretical workload variable, provided its interpretation is declared
and measured.
   Deﬁne a repair-workload unit as an empirically calibrated quantity of pathway-speciﬁc processing
demand. For example, an assay might estimate expected repair-factor occupancy time per lesion class,
or infer a distribution of processing time for a deﬁned lesion type under a speciﬁed cell condition. A
single heavy-ion event may generate a random total workload Sk , with distribution depending on
LET, species, cell state, and cluster architecture. If repair capacity varies over time, write µ(t). If
multiple pathways operate in parallel, the model requires a service network or vector capacity µ, not
a scalar.


6.3   Lindley workload recursion

For a restricted single-server abstraction, let An be the interarrival time between event workloads, let
Sn be service work, and let µ be constant service capacity measured in work units per unit time. The
unﬁnished work immediately before event n obeys

                                 Wn+1 = max{0, Wn + Sn − µAn+1 }.                                    (9)

This recursion is useful because it makes recovery debt explicit. When event spacing is long relative to
service demand, the queue empties. When multiple large workloads arrive close together, unﬁnished
work accumulates. The recurrence also clariﬁes why identical event counts can have diﬀerent outcomes:
the order and spacing of workloads matter.

distributed service requirements S, and constant service rate µ. Its traﬃc intensity is

Proposition 7.1 (Two-site dependence counterexample). Consider a system that fails if both of two
sites are disturbed. Let each site have marginal disturbance probability p ∈ (0, 1). Under independent
disturbances, the failure probability is p2 . Under a common-shock model in which both sites are dis-
turbed together with probability p, the marginal probabilities are still p, but the failure probability is p.
Thus equal marginals do not determine the failure probability.

Proof. For independent Bernoulli indicators X1 , X2 , P(X1 = X2 = 1) = P(X1 = 1)P(X2 = 1) = p2 .
Under the common-shock construction, set X1 = X2 = U where U ∼ Bernoulli(p). Each marginal
remains p, while P(X1 = X2 = 1) = P(U = 1) = p. Since p2 ̸= p for p ∈ (0, 1), the failure probabilities
diﬀer.

   This result does not say that positive correlation always increases every conceivable failure event.
The direction depends on the event geometry, code, and observable. It says that marginal calibration
alone does not identify a nonlinear failure functional. A matched-occupancy experiment must either
measure relevant dependence statistics or state that its comparison is one particular channel contrast.


7.2    Queue stability result

Proposition 7.2 (M/G/1 workload stability). Assume Poisson arrivals with rate λ > 0, iid nonneg-
ative service requirements Sk with ﬁnite mean, a work-conserving single server with constant service
rate µ > 0, and no abandonment or feedback. The standard M/G/1 workload process admits a proper
stationary workload distribution when ρ = λE[S]/µ < 1. When ρ ≥ 1, the oﬀered work is not below
service capacity and a ﬁnite stationary workload distribution does not exist under the usual nondegen-
erate assumptions.

Proof. Rescale service work into time units by deﬁning B = S/µ. The server then has unit service
speed and the M/G/1 traﬃc intensity is λE[B] = λE[S]/µ = ρ. The classical M/G/1 stability criterion
is ρ < 1, under the stated ﬁnite-mean assumptions. The proposition is the workload version of this
standard result; see, for example, Gross et al. [1998].

   For heavy-tailed workloads, the queue can be stable while its stationary workload has a long tail and
large quantiles. A ﬁnite mean does not guarantee a small probability of severe backlog. A practical
study should estimate not only E[S], but also the tail, variance, possible mixture structure, and
dependence of workload on prior events. If events arrive in clusters or if service capacity changes with
time, the M/G/1 criterion is no longer automatically suﬃcient. A Markov-modulated, batch-arrival,
or network-of-queues model may be required.

7.3   Catastrophic marked-event risk

Suppose disturbance arrivals are Poisson with rate λ. Each arrival independently receives a mark and
is catastrophic with probability qF , where catastrophe is deﬁned relative to a speciﬁed code/decoder
or biological endpoint. Independent thinning implies that catastrophic events form a Poisson process
with rate λqF .

Proposition 7.3 (Poisson thinning horizon risk). Under the preceding assumptions, the probability
of at least one catastrophic event in a horizon of length T ≥ 0 is

                                     Pcat (T ) = 1 − exp(−λqF T ).                                  (11)

Proof. The count of arrivals in the horizon is Poisson with mean λT . Independent marking retains
each event with probability qF , so the retained count is Poisson with mean λqF T . The probability of
zero retained events is exp(−λqF T ); its complement yields the result.

   The quantity qF is not a universal constant. It depends on the code or biological system, the
event mark distribution, operating conditions, recovery response, and chosen endpoint. For quantum
hardware, it could be the conditional probability that a radiation event drives a logical failure within
a speciﬁed correction horizon. For a cell, it could be the probability that the event causes a chosen
biological endpoint under speciﬁed exposure and repair conditions. Those probabilities must be
separately measured.

Combining this with queue stability gives two distinct constraints: one on the expected workload
oﬀered to a recovery process and one on the rate of events that individually cross a declared catas-
trophic boundary. Neither constraint subsumes the other. A system can have ρ < 1 but still experience
rare catastrophic events; conversely, an individual event may be non-catastrophic while the long-run
workload is unstable.

   Central result
   The safe operating region is a function of at least the event rate, the full mark distribution,
   footprint law, code or repair model, ﬁnite recovery capacity, observation horizon, and the ac-
   ceptable risk. No universal scalar “burst threshold” follows from average occupancy or dose
   alone.


7.5   Dimensionless descriptors for cross-domain comparison

Useful comparisons can be organized around dimensionless ratios, provided their domain-speciﬁc inter-
pretation is preserved. Examples include the expected number of events per recovery time λτR , burst
extent relative to code distance or characteristic repair-domain size L/d or L/ℓR , oﬀered workload
ratio ρ, and the number of correlated sites per event divided by the number of active components.
These ratios do not create a common physical unit. They help compare structural regimes such as
isolated disturbances, overlapping bursts, and recovery backlog.

8     Benchmark design: a transparent geometric surrogate
8.1   Why a surrogate is used

A physical quantum-code simulation and a biological track-structure simulation require diﬀerent
toolchains, parameter sets, and validation data. A surface-code study requires a speciﬁc stabilizer
layout, measurement circuit, detector error model, decoder, and ﬁnite-size scaling. A radiobiology
study requires physical track transport and chemical/biological response models. The present experi-
ment isolates a narrower mathematical question: can two channels with the same expected lattice-wide
mean occupancy have diﬀerent spanning-connectivity statistics when one channel has spatially corre-
lated ﬁnite footprints?
   The lattice is a ﬁnite square graph GN = (VN , EN ) with N × N sites and four-neighbour edges.
A site is declared erased or occupied by a disturbance. Failure is deﬁned as the existence of a four-
neighbour connected component touching opposite boundaries, either left-to-right or bottom-to-top.
This event is a deliberately simple geometric proxy for a connected failure set; it is not an encoded
logical failure.


8.2   IID channel

For target probability p, each lattice site is independently erased with probability p:

This is a homogeneous baseline with identical sitewise marginals and no spatial covariance between
distinct sites.


8.3   Finite-track channel

A track is a straight segment of ﬁve lattice sites centered at a uniformly selected anchor and oriented
uniformly among four directions: horizontal, vertical, and the two diagonals. A Poisson number of
tracks is superposed on the lattice. Tracks are clipped at boundaries; if several tracks cover the same
site, it is still counted once. Let qi denote the probability that one uniformly anchored and oriented
track covers site i. For a Poisson number of independent track placements with intensity λT , Poisson
thinning gives

is not site-by-site equality with the IID model. It is exact equality of expected lattice-wide mean
occupancy:

10     Interpreting the reliability envelope
The mathematical layers provide a compact way to state what must be known before a system-level
reliability prediction can be made. First, the event process must be characterized. Second, event
marks must be translated into a substrate-speciﬁc disturbance. Third, recovery and observation must
be measured. Fourth, the failure event and horizon must be deﬁned. A statement such as “the average
event rate is low” addresses only part of the problem.


10.1   Quantum operating envelope

For quantum devices, a candidate operating envelope is a set of code/decoder/channel conﬁgurations
satisfying both logical-risk and real-time constraints:

                                        AQ = {(d, η, τ ) : PL (d, η; T ) ≤ ϵL ,    P(decode latency > τ ) ≤ ϵτ } .          (16)

Here PL should be measured under event-conditioned channels and include relevant correlations across
qubits and cycles. The latency constraint accounts for the real-time nature of fault-tolerant control.
The model could include detector-informed mitigation, shielding, material changes, and code/decoder
co-design, but those interventions must be evaluated on the same device and benchmark protocol.

10.3                  Why matched dose and matched occupancy are diﬀerent

Dose matching is a physical exposure design choice. Occupancy matching is a statistical construc-
tion in a simpliﬁed model. A real experiment could match absorbed dose while observing diﬀerent
track distributions, or match the mean number of qubit error events while observing diﬀerent spatial
correlation. The variables being matched must be stated. For a fair quantum comparison, matching
only the average bit-ﬂip rate may still leave diﬀerent leakage rates and time correlations. For a fair
biological comparison, matching dose may still leave diﬀerent lesion multiplicity and chemistry. In
both cases, a richer calibration vector is needed.

an apparent burst may be an alignment artifact or an unmodeled confounder.

11.1                             Quantum measurement protocol

A quantum study should collect: (i) radiation detector events with calibrated spatial response and
time stamps; (ii) qubit-resolved error or coherence measurements; (iii) device layout and material-
stack metadata; (iv) cryogenic operating variables and pulse-tube/vibration channels; (v) stabilizer
or syndrome outcomes; and (vi) decoder output and latency. The primary event model should be ﬁtted
on one set of runs and evaluated on held-out runs, devices, or operating conditions. Event-triggered
averages should be complemented by matched control windows and shuﬄed-time null models. The
analysis should report detector eﬃciency, event association uncertainty, missing data, and false alarms.
   A fair decoder comparison should hold ﬁxed the code family, code distance, syndrome schedule,
decoder compute budget, logical observable, and correction deadline. Baselines should include an IID
model, a physically motivated correlated model, and an oracle model only when the oracle information
is deﬁned and available in the experiment. If a detector signal is used as side information, the latency
and resource use of that detector should be counted. A decoder that achieves a lower logical error by
consuming information that arrives too late is not an operational improvement.


11.2                             Biological measurement protocol

A biological study should combine physical track-structure estimates with lesion-sensitive microscopy
or molecular measurements and explicit repair time courses. Radiation quality, ion species, en-
ergy, dose, dose rate, cell type, cell-cycle distribution, oxygenation, and genomic context should
be recorded. Candidate endpoints include clustered DSB foci, lesion complexity, repair-factor recruit-
ment, sequencing-based repair outcomes, chromosome aberrations, and clonogenic survival. These
endpoints should be preregistered and analysed separately.
   Track simulations can provide a prior for the spatial deposition kernel, but the biological trans-
formation from energy deposition to lesion complexity should be calibrated with empirical assays.
Where possible, uncertainty from the track simulator, chemical model, image reconstruction, and cell-
to-cell heterogeneity should be propagated into the ﬁnal repair-workload distribution. A single mean

11.3             Calibration, holdout, and falsiﬁcation

The validation sequence should be: deﬁne an observable; calibrate the event detector; estimate a

cluster statistics; then evaluate the downstream decoder or repair endpoint. At each layer, a null
model and a competing explanation are needed. In quantum systems, vibration and thermal drift are
plausible confounders. In biological systems, baseline damage, cell-cycle heterogeneity, oxygenation,
and cell-speciﬁc repair competence are plausible confounders.

the observation model and control objective are deﬁned. An AI system cannot compensate for an

remaining wrong under distribution shift.

12.3            Observability and safe exploration

A central issue is partial observability. A ﬂuorescent damage marker is not the entire lesion state.
A detector coincidence is not the complete radiation ﬁeld. If an adaptive algorithm uses a proxy as
though it were ground truth, it can allocate resources to the wrong event class. An oﬄine analysis
should ﬁrst test whether the available measurements identify the state variables used by the proposed
policy. In an experimental biological setting, exploration must be constrained by biosafety and ethics
review, and no AI-generated schedule should be interpreted as a clinical prescription.

13                             Quantum sensors in living cells: capability and boundary
The 2026 Nature study by Abrahams and colleagues reports engineered magneto-sensitive ﬂuorescent
proteins, including MagLOV variants, with optically detected magnetic resonance in living bacterial
cells at room temperature and single-cell sensitivity [Abrahams et al., 2026]. The paper demonstrates a
platform for quantum-enabled biosensing and spatial localization, using spin-dependent photophysics
associated with a radical-pair mechanism. It is a signiﬁcant example of quantum measurement in a
biological environment.
   The correct interpretation is that engineered proteins can support biological quantum sensing. This
does not establish that a bacterium is a fault-tolerant quantum computer, that living cells implement
holographic quantum error correction, or that the reported sensor repairs radiation-induced DNA
lesions. Sensing a magnetic or molecular environment is not the same task as encoding and protecting
an arbitrary quantum logical state. The distinction matters because a shared vocabulary of quantum
states, noise, or coherence can invite unjustiﬁed extrapolation across very diﬀerent experimental
systems.
    A possible future cross-domain measurement programme could use quantum sensors to study local
environments in cells, while separate radiation and lesion assays quantify track-induced damage. The
sensor’s own photophysics, excitation conditions, temperature, redox state, and cellular expression
would require calibration. The measurement instrument must not be assumed to be a passive observer
if its illumination or chemistry changes the cell state.

15     Limitations, failure modes, and open questions
The framework is intentionally broad, but its components are not yet jointly calibrated. The prin-
cipal limitation is that the two substrate maps remain external to the synthetic benchmark. The
lattice model measures geometric connectedness and does not contain a quantum code or biological
mechanism. Its value is to isolate dependence eﬀects under a transparent construction.


15.1    Model-form limitations

The track law uses a ﬁxed length, four orientations, uniform anchors, Poisson-distributed event counts,
and hard clipping. Physical tracks have energy-dependent radial dose proﬁles, secondary-electron
structure, species-dependent ranges, and stochastic chemistry. A superconducting device’s distur-
bance footprint depends on materials, geometry, phonon transport, quasiparticle dynamics, and mea-
surement circuits. The common mark vector is a placeholder for these richer descriptions. The selected
Poisson parent process is not a ﬁt to measured particle arrivals or correlated quasiparticle clusters.


15.2    Statistical limitations

The primary sweep has 4,000 trials per point and a ﬁnite grid of ten occupancy values. The Wilson
intervals account for Monte Carlo binomial uncertainty but not uncertainty about the generator or
calibration. The sensitivity runs have smaller sample sizes. The ﬁnite-size analysis is too coarse for
critical-exponent estimation and should not be read as a percolation threshold result [Grimmett, 1999].
The extra track-length study changes only one synthetic mark and leaves all other assumptions ﬁxed.
The covariance ﬁgure is directional and conditional on the chosen track geometry.


15.3    Queueing limitations

The M/G/1 result assumes Poisson arrivals, iid service times, one work-conserving server, constant ca-
pacity, and no interactions between jobs beyond waiting. Cells have multiple coupled repair pathways
and active regulation; decoders are parallel pipelines with ﬁnite deadlines, not necessarily single-server
queues. A more realistic model may require a vector service network, state-dependent arrival process,

15.4    Causal limitations

A correlation between detector events and qubit observables does not by itself identify all mediating

or genomic outcome. Confounding and selection eﬀects must be controlled. Proposed interventions
such as shielding, device material modiﬁcations, targeted ROS perturbations, or repair-resource allo-
cation are not established by the mathematical framework.


15.5    Ethical and experimental limitations

No human or animal data, patient data, live-cell intervention, radiation exposure, or quantum-
hardware measurement were carried out for this manuscript. The proposed experimental work would
require suitable facility access, radiation safety review, institutional biosafety oversight, ethical ap-
proval where applicable, and qualiﬁed domain scientists. Synthetic code is not a substitute for those
procedures.


16     Falsiﬁable research programme
The framework is useful only if it makes testable predictions. The following hypotheses are not
assumed to be true; they deﬁne experiments that could reject or reﬁne the proposed abstraction.


16.1    Quantum hypotheses
1. Event-conditioned predictive gain. A model using detector event geometry and time marks
   should predict held-out qubit error maps better than an IID model at matched mean error rate.
   Falsiﬁcation occurs if the gain disappears under held-out runs or after conditioning on vibration
   and cryogenic variables.
2. Decoder transfer. A decoder trained on calibrated burst data should reduce logical error or
   improve calibration under the measured correlated channel without violating latency limits. Falsi-
   ﬁcation occurs if the advantage vanishes under device/run holdout or requires information arriving
   too late.

                                                     26
BURST-ROBUST CODES                                                            PREPRINT • 06 NOV 2026



3. Code geometry dependence. Diﬀerent code geometries should exhibit diﬀerent logical-risk
   responses to the same measured event footprint. The claim is falsiﬁed for a particular comparison
   if code geometry has no detectable eﬀect within uncertainty under the speciﬁed channel.


16.2    Biological hypotheses
1. Track-aware lesion prediction. A track-structure model should predict held-out lesion-cluster
   statistics better than a dose-only model at matched absorbed dose. Falsiﬁcation occurs if the
   track-aware model provides no reproducible predictive gain.
2. Workload-tail relevance. A workload distribution including lesion complexity and pathway-
   speciﬁc processing times should predict persistence endpoints better than lesion count alone. Fal-
   siﬁcation occurs if the workload representation adds no information beyond simpler predictors.
3. Perturbation transportability. dCas9–KillerRed may calibrate selected localized oxidative
   damage and repair observables, but transfer to high-LET tracks must be tested and is not as-
   sumed. Falsiﬁcation of transportability is expected if the lesion chemistry, cluster scale, or pathway
   response diﬀers materially.


16.3    Cross-domain hypothesis

A restricted common statistical model may improve forecasting when it preserves event marks, foot-
print dependence, and recovery workload. It should not be judged by whether the same ﬁtted pa-
rameters apply to both domains. The commonality is structural: each domain supplies its own mark
distribution, kernel, recovery model, and endpoint. If a shared representation does not improve calibra-
tion or predictive eﬃciency over domain-speciﬁc baselines, the cross-domain layer has little practical
value beyond exposition.


17     Conclusion
Spatially extended events challenge reliability models that retain only an average disturbance rate.
This paper sets out a layered mathematical framework in which marked events generate domain-
speciﬁc footprints, substrate maps convert those footprints into quantum errors or biological lesions,
and recovery processes determine whether disturbances become system-level failures. The framework
keeps event statistics, physical damage, decoding or repair, and observable-speciﬁc reliability separate.
   The mathematical results are deliberately modest and explicit. Equal marginal occupancy does
not determine a nonlinear failure probability. A standard M/G/1 workload model is stable when its
traﬃc intensity is below one. Poisson thinning gives a closed-form horizon risk when catastrophic
marks are independently assigned. These facts can help structure a reliability envelope, but none is
a universal threshold for a quantum computer or a cell.
   The ﬁnite-lattice benchmark reproduces the original 24×24 comparison and expands it with exact
expected mean-occupancy calibration, uncertainty intervals, covariance and component-size diagnos-
tics, ﬁnite-size sensitivity, and track-length sensitivity. Its central observation is speciﬁc: under the
chosen ﬁve-site Poisson line-track generator and four-neighbour spanning criterion, the track process
has a higher observed spanning frequency than IID erasures at every tested target occupancy. The cor-
rect interpretation is a warning against relying on mean occupancy alone, not an empirical statement
about actual quantum or biological systems.
   A stronger next study must replace the synthetic track law with calibrated device or radiation-
track data, deﬁne a concrete holographic or stabilizer code, and validate biological workloads against
measured lesion and repair endpoints. Neural decoding and adaptive resource allocation are valuable
research directions only after event observability, endpoint validity, latency, and safety constraints

                                                   27
BURST-ROBUST CODES                                                             PREPRINT • 06 NOV 2026



have been speciﬁed. Finally, the neutrino fog remains an analogy about statistical inference under
background limitations in dark-matter detection; it is not a cellular repair ﬂoor.

    Central result
    The defensible conclusion is methodological: reliability under burst-like disturbances is a joint
    property of event geometry, substrate response, recovery resources, and the chosen failure end-
    point. Average error rate, absorbed dose, or mean occupancy alone is generally not enough to
    identify that joint property.

D      Minimum data schema for a future calibrated event record
A future quantum-device event record should include: event identiﬁer; UTC or synchronized mono-
tonic timestamp; detector identity and position; event class and posterior class probability; coincidence
multiplicity; estimated event location and uncertainty; qubit layout version; qubit-resolved response
windows; syndrome cycle index; operating temperature and pressure; vibration and pulse-tube fea-
tures; device materials; decoder version; decoder latency; and all exclusion ﬂags. Raw event times
and derived features should both be retained.
   A future radiation-track record should include: ion species; kinetic energy per nucleon; LET and its
deﬁnition; direction; entry coordinate; dose and dose rate; track-structure simulator/version; radial
dose proﬁle; secondary-electron statistics; nucleus and chromatin model assumptions; lesion class
and multiplicity; assay protocol; cell type and cell-cycle state; repair observation times; endpoint
deﬁnitions; and uncertainty or quality-control ﬂags. If a lesion workload is inferred, the transformation
from lesion class to workload must be documented separately from the physical track record.
