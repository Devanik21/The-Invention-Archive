Aging as a Decoding Threshold:
Repair Scheduling Under Fixed Maintenance Throughput
Devanik Debnath
Department of Electronics and Communication Engineering, National Institute of Technology Agartala, India
GitHub: Devanik21 | ORCID record supplied by author

Abstract
Cells accumulate heterogeneous lesions while repair pathways have finite throughput. This paper tests a narrow version
of the hypothesis that repair order can alter a threshold-defined failure time even when the offered repair budget is fixed. We
construct a deterministic four-state damage-propagation model with a nonnegative interaction matrix and periodically allocate
the same repair capacity to each of four abstract damage classes. All 24 class orders are enumerated across six coupling strengths;
every order gives each class the same nominal repair dose per cycle. In the decoupled limit, order is analytically irrelevant.
Under nonzero coupling, order changes first-passage time, but the effect is modest in the illustrative parameter range and a
continuous equal-share policy is generally competitive or better. A 10% increase in offered repair changes the threshold time
more than the difference between schedules in these simulations. The results support a conditional mathematical statement—not
a theory of organismal lifespan extension. We distinguish the coding-threshold analogy from literal error-correcting codes, and
identify tropical scheduling, percolation, stochastic control, ultrametric clustering, and reinforcement learning as candidate
extensions that require their own operational definitions and evidence.

Keywords: aging; damage propagation; repair scheduling; first-passage time; control theory; threshold models; reproducibility

1

Question and contribution

Aging is associated with changes across proteostasis, mitochondrial quality control, genome maintenance, and gene-regulatory
state. These processes interact, and the timing of interventions can matter when repair is delayed, state-dependent, or coupled to
downstream damage. Control-theoretic approaches to aging networks and optimal maintenance schedules already exist; in
particular, Sun, Michaels, and Mahadevan studied longevity optimization in interdependent networks and repair allocation [1].
Control-theoretic perspectives on aging predate that work [2]. The broad proposition that maintenance schedules can affect
longevity is therefore not novel by itself.
The present paper asks a more constrained question: in a transparent model, does the ordering of repair jobs alter a thresholdcrossing time when the total offered capacity and per-class dose per cycle are fixed? We also quantify the comparison against an
equal-share policy and a modest increase in repair capacity. The result is intentionally model-first and counterexample-oriented.
It does not infer that an organism has a literal biological decoder, or that changing pathway priority will extend lifespan.
The main contributions are: (i) a compact model with explicit variables, threshold, and scheduling constraints; (ii) exhaustive
enumeration of 24 periodic repair orders at matched nominal throughput; (iii) a limiting-case result and numerical convergence
checks; and (iv) a falsification-oriented interpretation that separates schedule-order effects from total-budget effects. This
release makes no empirical claim and uses no animal, patient, cell-line, or comparative-species dataset.

Prior work and scope

2.1

Aging control and maintenance

The network-optimal-control literature directly addresses repair scheduling and longevity trade-offs [1]. Our contribution is
not a replacement for this framework. It narrows the explanatory object to a controlled comparison in which nominal repair
throughput is fixed and every class receives identical cumulative offered dose over a cycle. The paper’s small system is designed
to expose when scheduling order has an effect and when it does not.

2.2

Biological interactions relevant to calibration

Mitophagy and mitochondrial biogenesis are coupled in C. elegans; perturbing one process can alter the other, so “repair flux”
cannot safely be treated as a single independent actuator without measurement [3]. Autophagy regulation varies with age, tissue,
and physiological context [4]. Proteostasis, degradation, senescence, and organismal aging also have complex relationships [5].
These studies motivate interactions but do not calibrate the abstract matrix used here.
The Information Theory of Aging discusses loss of youthful epigenetic information as a proposed aging mechanism [6]. The
present “decoding threshold” phrasing is a separate operational analogy: it means an externally defined functional boundary
in a state space. It does not assert that the cell is a digital communication system or that coding theory derives the biological
threshold.

Numerical protocol

We enumerate every permutation π ∈ S4 for κ ∈ {0, 0.25, 0.5, 0.75, 1, 1.25}. The system is integrated using fixed-step fourthorder Runge–Kutta with ∆t = 0.02; this step divides the 3-unit slot exactly. First-passage time is linearly interpolated across
the threshold crossing. We compare (i) all 24 scheduled orders, (ii) continuous equal-share repair at the same offered total
flux, and (iii) continuous equal-share repair with a 10% larger total offered flux. The third comparison is deliberately not a
matched-budget comparison; it measures whether a small budget increase can dominate the scheduling effect.

Interpretation and theoretical boundaries

6.1

The decoding-threshold analogy

The term “decoding threshold” can be useful only if the failure criterion is operational. In this work it is the scalar boundary
1⊤ x = θ , not a threshold theorem for biological codes. Classical error correction depends on a defined code, channel, error
model, and decoder; none is identified here. A biological application would need a measurable functional state, a task-specific
criterion for loss of function, and evidence that distinct lesions combine nonlinearly around that criterion.

6.2

Max-plus and tropical geometry

Max-plus algebra is natural for discrete-event systems with precedence constraints, in which completion of a job depends on
the maximum completion time among its predecessors [7]. It predicts a scheduling bottleneck only if pathway or repair-task
dependencies can be written as actual prerequisites with identifiable processing times. Figure 6 is therefore a vocabulary
illustration, not a biological discovery. A falsifiable quantitative prediction would be that the measured critical path predicts
repair completion delay better than a sum-of-times baseline, out of sample.

6.3

Percolation and damage correlation

Percolation theory offers tools to study when connected damaged regions lose connectivity [8]. But the current model has no
spatial lattice or measured damage clusters; κ scales causal cross-state propagation and is not a correlation coefficient. A next
study could compare threshold time with a cluster-percolation order parameter on image-registered mitochondrial networks or
chromatin domains. It should test whether cluster statistics add predictive value beyond total damage and local burden.

6.4

Stochastic control and reinforcement learning

An optimal-control formulation could minimize expected first-passage time, E[τ f ], while limiting total repair capacity. A
proper stochastic model would require process noise, state-dependent repair effectiveness, observation noise, and a justified
policy constraint. Reinforcement learning is relevant only after a calibrated environment exists and must be compared with
a non-learning policy, the uniform baseline, and an exact small-state solution where available [9]. No RL training result is
claimed in this paper.

6.5

Ultrametrics and damage lineages

An ultrametric distance might be appropriate for damage states that descend through a branching lineage, but only if a lineage
tree or ancestry-aware distance is measured rather than imposed. P-adic language is not used as decorative machinery here
because no biological observation currently makes that extra structure necessary. A meaningful future test would ask whether
lineage-derived ultrametric clustering predicts the best repair priority across held-out perturbations after controlling for burden,
cell identity, and lineage depth.

7

Falsification and experimental plan

A realistic validation programme should begin with a tractable system such as C. elegans or yeast, but only after directly
measuring the candidate actuator and total repair activity. The proposed experiment is not performed in this release. A minimal
study would: (1) characterize autophagy and mitophagy reporters and their response delays; (2) estimate the interaction matrix
and threshold using independently measured perturbations; (3) randomize two or more pathway-priority schedules while
matching integrated repair flux and per-pathway dose; (4) pre-register a functional endpoint and a minimum relevant effect size;
and (5) report survival curves, uncertainty intervals, flux matching error, and off-target effects. A reporter-derived flux proxy is
not automatically the amount of functional damage removed.
The hypothesis that schedule order helps in this context is falsified if order effects are absent within a pre-specified equivalence
margin despite sufficient temporal resolution and verified flux matching, or if any apparent benefit disappears after controlling
for total activity, delay, toxicity, and baseline damage. A null result with wide uncertainty is inconclusive. If continuous
equal-share or a simple rule-based schedule equals or beats a learned policy on held-out data, the claimed advantage of AI
scheduling fails. In multicellular models, lineage-level damage tracing and species-specific calibration would remain major
sources of uncertainty.

Limitations

The model is deliberately small and deterministic. Damage is assumed to separate into four nonnegative classes with constant
source and linear interactions, while the repair input has no observation noise, delays, toxicity, saturation, or state-dependent
effectiveness. The failure threshold is a scalar sum and omits compensatory mechanisms, cell turnover, organismal physiology,
and competing risks. The matrix and all numerical constants are synthetic, dimensionless choices. Thus the numerical range is
a proof-of-implementation for this toy model, not a prediction of days of life, percent lifespan extension, or treatment effect.
Even the “matched flux” comparator matches offered repair capacity; it does not represent measured realized biochemical flux
under a real pathway.
The tropical, percolation, ultrametric, HJB, and RL concepts are not integrated into the numerical core because doing
so without data or independently validated assumptions would be mathematical decoration. The paper gives each one an
operational prerequisite and a falsification route instead. The topic’s broader claim that optimal scheduling can extend lifespan
remains open and requires experiments and model calibration that are outside this release.

9

Conclusion

Repair order can affect threshold-crossing time in an interacting damage model even when the offered total repair rate and
dose per class per cycle are fixed. In the specified four-state example, however, the observed order effect is conditional and
comparatively modest; a continuous equal-share policy is competitive, and a 10% budget increase can produce a larger change.
The decoupled limit provides an exact null case in which order is irrelevant. These results do not prove that repair scheduling
extends lifespan. They motivate a more restrictive experimental question: after verifying interactions, delay, and matched
pathway activity, does repair order improve a predefined functional threshold or survival endpoint over a simple equal-share
baseline?
