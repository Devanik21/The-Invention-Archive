# Problem 1: Emergence of biological information from nonliving chemistry

Question. How did nonliving chemical networks become information-bearing, self-reproducing and evolvable? This is more specific than how the first RNA appeared. We ask under what conditions a catalytic chemical system carries heritable catalytic information, and whether that differs from simple self-maintenance.

Key background, as summarised during the literature search (not exhaustive):

- Prebiotic chemical space is far larger than the few thousand molecules used by extant biochemistry. Network closure is a unifying concept for selectable chemical units.
- RAF (reflexively autocatalytic, food-generated) theory formalises collectively autocatalytic sets. Detecting a RAF is efficient; finding a smallest RAF is NP-hard.
- Carter and Wills propose that coding arose from the interaction of two collectively autocatalytic sets, one nucleic and one peptide.
- Iizuka (2026, preprint under review) proposes a stacked-autoencoder analogy for hierarchical information in self-replication. Its claims are qualitative.

The gap we are testing is whether catalytic self-maintenance (RAF membership) implies heritable catalytic information. Established results supply the background; the separation is the open question.

Sub-projects: [PCT-I](PCT-I_Hereditary_Catalytic_Capacity/), [PCT-II](PCT-II_Hierarchical_Information_Architecture/), [PCT-III](PCT-III_Multigenerational_Persistence/).

Current conclusion. In the minimal model tested in PCT-I, RAF membership coincides with operational heredity, and heredity decays gradually with copying error rather than being lost within three generations. The separation was not found. See PCT-I for details and limitations.
