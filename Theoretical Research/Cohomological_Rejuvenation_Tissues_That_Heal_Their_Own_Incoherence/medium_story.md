# Cohomological Rejuvenation: Can a Tissue Measure Its Own Incoherence?

## Aging is not only a cell-intrinsic problem

Aging is often described through damage that accumulates inside cells: mutations, epigenetic drift, mitochondrial dysfunction, loss of proteostasis, senescence, and altered metabolism. These mechanisms matter, but a tissue is not just a bag of independent cells. Its function depends on spatial organization, cell-type composition, extracellular structure, and communication among neighboring cells. A neuron can retain a plausible transcriptional state while its glial and vascular neighborhood changes. A stromal cell may behave differently depending on which immune cells surround it. Individual cells can look reasonable in isolation while their relationships become less coordinated.

This local-to-global framing is compelling, but a mathematical word such as *cohomology* should not be used as a decorative synonym for disorder. The core question is stricter: can we define two separate quantities for local mismatch and global compatibility obstruction, measure them from spatial data, and show that they add information beyond existing aging markers?

There is already important prior work. A 2026 article by Juan J. Segura explicitly models aging as cross-hallmark obstruction amplification using sheaf-theoretic language, and a 2026 preprint by Wang and Wei develops hierarchical sheaf-spectral representations for single-cell RNA sequencing. Cellular sheaf Laplacians themselves have a developed mathematical literature. This means the broad idea of using sheaves to analyze aging or biological networks cannot responsibly be presented as an unprecedented invention. The opportunity is to make the measurement claims precise and define a test that could invalidate them.

## A tissue graph, with a careful definition of agreement

Imagine a spatial graph whose nodes are measured cells and whose edges connect selected neighbors. Each cell carries a vector of features: perhaps a carefully chosen gene-expression representation, signaling-associated measurements, or other state variables. Crucially, neighboring cells should not be expected to have identical vectors. A microglial cell and a neuron have different roles by design. Agreement therefore means compatibility under an explicitly specified relationship, not equality.

For each edge, define linear maps that send the states of the two cells into a common comparison space. Their difference is the edge discrepancy. Stacking these discrepancies produces a coboundary operator, written as \(B\). The maps may be estimated from training data, calibrated against measured response features, or constructed from a prespecified model. Each choice makes assumptions and each must be evaluated separately. Ligand-receptor co-expression, for example, is not by itself a complete measurement of a signaling channel.

The associated sheaf Laplacian is

$$
L_0 = B^\top W B,
$$

where \(W\) is a diagonal matrix of nonnegative edge weights. Its most important property is elementary and exact:

$$
x^\top L_0 x = \|W^{1/2} Bx\|_2^2 \geq 0.
$$

This identity gives a nonnegative energy measuring mismatch relative to the chosen compatibility maps. Call it local mismatch, not rejuvenation. A low value means the representation fits the maps; it does not mean that the tissue is young or functional. A high value can also reflect noise, a mistaken map, cell-type composition, spatial density, or a poorly constructed graph.

## The important distinction: energy is not cohomology

The tempting move is to say that high sheaf energy is a high first-cohomology obstruction. That is not generally correct. The edge residual \(Bx\) is generated by applying the coboundary operator to a state. It therefore lies in the image of \(B\) by construction. It is a mismatch, but not an irreducible cohomological obstruction.

Cohomology asks a different question: is an observed pattern of edge relationships compatible with any global assignment of states under the specified maps? On a graph, take an observed edge cochain \(y\). Its component that cannot be represented as a coboundary can be isolated using an orthogonal projection onto the image of \(B\). With \(A=W^{1/2}B\) and \(P_A=AA^\dagger\), the residual energy is

$$
D_{\mathrm{cyc}} = \|(I-P_A)W^{1/2}y\|_2^2.
$$

This has a cycle-level interpretation in a graph-only complex. For a higher-dimensional complex with triangles or other faces, cohomology also requires the relevant closure condition; one cannot simply project arbitrary data and call the result a cohomology class. A real analysis must specify the edge observation \(y\), the complex, the maps, and the conditions under which its obstruction is meaningful.

This distinction leads to two separate statistics: local mismatch for a tissue state, and cycle-level incompatibility for observed edge relationships. Combining them into one number may be useful later, but only after a principled normalization and empirical validation. It should not be a starting assumption.

## What current data make possible

Spatial aging datasets make this hypothesis testable in principle. Sun and colleagues reported a mouse-brain MERFISH atlas with roughly 4.2 million cells sampled across 20 ages, and analysed cell-proximity effects in aging and rejuvenation. Another study mapped age-associated gene expression across spatial domains of the mouse brain. These resources are promising because cell positions, tissue regions, and age annotations can be used to build spatial graphs and test whether local relationships carry information missed by cell-intrinsic averages.

But a large number of cells does not equal a large number of independent aging observations. The biological replicate is the animal or donor. If millions of cells from a small number of mice are split randomly between training and test sets, a model can memorize donor-specific traits and appear more accurate than it is. The primary validation must hold out whole animals or donors, fit preprocessing and compatibility maps within training folds, and report uncertainty at the biological-replicate level.

A credible computational study would begin with an age-prediction target and explicit covariates: cell type, anatomical region, sex, assay batch, sequencing quality, spatial density, and graph-construction parameters. The sheaf features would then be compared against transparent baselines, including cell-type composition, standard neighborhood statistics, graph Laplacian features, and cell-intrinsic age estimators where compatible modalities are available. A claimed benefit should be an improvement on held-out donors, not merely a significant correlation in the full dataset.

The null models matter just as much as the positive model. Shuffle spatial coordinates within anatomical regions. Rewire edges while preserving node degrees. Match cell-type composition. Randomize restriction maps. Compare same-cell-type neighbors with selected cross-type interactions. Vary graph radius and downsample the data. If the score still predicts age after these checks, the finding becomes more interesting. If a random map performs just as well, the proposed interpretation has failed.

## The hypothesis must be allowed to die

The proposed biomarker idea should be rejected or narrowed if its age association disappears after adjustment, fails when entire donors are held out, changes sign under reasonable graph choices, or mostly recapitulates cell composition or spatial density. An age-associated score is also not automatically a predictor of frailty, mortality, function, or response to rejuvenation. Those claims require cohorts with the appropriate outcomes and separate validation.

This is the immediate value of a rigorous formulation: it turns an alluring metaphor into a research question with failure conditions. The first computational result could be negative, and it would still be useful. It would show that this particular sheaf representation does not provide independent information under the tested conditions.

## Could a tissue repair its own mismatch?

The more speculative part of the proposal imagines a controller that uses local discrepancy as feedback and attempts to adjust a tissue toward a reference distribution. Schrödinger bridges offer a framework for finding a path measure connecting two endpoint distributions while minimizing relative entropy with respect to a chosen reference process. Related methods are already used in single-cell trajectory inference. But that objective is not automatically identical to thermodynamic entropy production. A physical interpretation requires a justified model of the reference dynamics and a mathematical relation between the path-space cost and thermodynamic dissipation.

Partial epigenetic reprogramming is also an existing research area, not a newly invented actuator. Its promise is accompanied by serious issues around preserving cell identity and preventing inappropriate proliferation or dedifferentiation. A theoretical proposal must not suggest that using fewer factors or shorter pulses automatically makes reprogramming safe.

Synthetic Notch, or synNotch, circuits allow engineered cells to sense specific cell-contact ligands and trigger programmed transcriptional responses. That is powerful, but it does not mean a cell can directly compute an abstract, high-dimensional sheaf residual from transcriptomic data. Any future circuit would need a measurable molecular proxy, an experimentally calibrated mapping from proxy to the target statistic, and extensive evidence that the response remains bounded and preserves cell identity. Those are unresolved steps, not engineering details that can be assumed away.

An AI agent could help test candidate controllers in simulation, quantify uncertainty, or choose the next informative dataset. It cannot overcome unidentifiable signals or turn correlation into causation. The sensible order is therefore computational biomarker validation first, intervention design much later, and biological deployment only after independent validation and oversight.

## The actual contribution

The central contribution of this proposal is narrower than its title may first suggest. It separates a valid local energy from a cohomological obstruction, records the assumptions required for each, and proposes a donor-held-out test using existing spatial aging data. It also corrects a common overstatement about Schrödinger bridges and makes clear that current contact-sensing circuits do not measure arbitrary learned tissue discrepancies.

No biological atlas was analysed for this release, and the included code verifies only finite-dimensional mathematical identities and a synthetic pattern deliberately inserted into a toy model. It provides no evidence that real tissue mismatch rises with age. The preprint should be judged by that standard rather than by the attractiveness of the cross-disciplinary synthesis.

If the analysis eventually finds a stable signal that survives spatial, cell-type, technical, and donor-level null models—and adds predictive information beyond established baselines—then a tissue-scale compatibility statistic may deserve further study. If not, it should be discarded. That is the point of calling the idea falsifiable.
