Abstract
Epigenetic clocks are powerful predictors of chronological age and several health outcomes, but a
predictor is not automatically a manipulable measure of functional aging. Targeted methylation editing
can perturb clock-associated sites and produce genome-wide bystander changes; genetic variation may also
influence some clock CpGs. These observations motivate a precise question: when an intervention lowers a
clock score, what evidence identifies a change in latent functional state rather than a direct change in the
measurement channel? We formulate a minimal state-space model with a functional-aging coordinate, a
clock-sensitive molecular coordinate, an inherited set point, and an identity/safety coordinate. We prove
an observational-equivalence result: a single scalar clock contrast cannot separate functional repair from
direct marker displacement without additional design variation or an independent outcome. We derive
rank conditions for identification, show why paired measurements remove an additive time-invariant genetic
set point but not genotype-by-treatment interactions, and construct a constrained sequential experiment
planner that maximizes expected information gain while penalizing identity risk and measurement cost. In
a deterministic synthetic benchmark, a marker-only perturbation produces a clock reduction of 0.300 units
while the functional endpoint changes by -0.009 units; the apparent clock-only “repair” rule is triggered in
1.000 of simulated trials. These values are outputs of the released model, not biological measurements. The
contribution is a falsifiable design principle and reproducible stress test, not a new biological mechanism or
evidence that any intervention rejuvenates living tissue.
Core result. A clock change is an observation, not a causal diagnosis. Identification requires independently
varied perturbations, at least one functional readout with a defensible measurement model, and explicit
checks for genotype and identity effects.
Keywords: epigenetic clocks; causal identifiability; methylation editing; biological age; active experimental
design; information geometry; synthetic benchmark; rejuvenation safety.
Scope and status. This paper is a theoretical and synthetic-computational preprint. All new numerical
values are generated from the stylized model in verify_model.py. No public biological dataset, wet-lab
experiment, clinical intervention, or organismal lifespan assay was performed for this release. The results
do not establish rejuvenation efficacy or safety.

Introduction
DNA-methylation clocks compress many molecular measurements into a scalar that predicts age or age-associated
outcomes. Horvath’s multi-tissue clock established a widely used framework for age prediction across tissues
and cell types [Horvath, 2013]; later clocks were designed to improve associations with healthspan, mortality, or
physiological phenotypes [Levine et al., 2018, Lu et al., 2019]. Their predictive usefulness makes them attractive
endpoints for interventions intended to reverse aging-associated molecular changes. Yet the interpretation of a
changed score is more difficult than the prediction task that produced it.
The distinction is not merely philosophical. In 2025, Liesenfelder and colleagues reported that targeted
epigenetic editing at individual age-associated CpGs could evoke genome-wide bystander methylation changes and
alter epigenetic-clock outputs [Liesenfelder et al., 2025]. In 2026, a systematic transcription-factor perturbation
platform identified several candidate rejuvenating perturbations while explicitly seeking to avoid dedifferentiation
[Sengstack et al., 2026]. A 2026 preprint, Germline-Informed Aging, further reported enrichment of methylation
quantitative-trait loci among clock CpGs, raising the possibility that inherited differences contribute to some
between-person clock variation [Lim, 2026]. These results do not invalidate clocks. They sharpen the causal
question: a measurement can be predictive, responsive to intervention, and still fail to identify which latent
process changed.
We study a narrow but consequential failure mode. Suppose an intervention directly shifts methylation at
clock-sensitive loci while leaving functional state unchanged. The clock can move even if repair does not occur.
Conversely, a functional improvement may be obscured by a marker response in the opposite direction. A
scalar endpoint alone cannot resolve these alternatives unless additional assumptions or measurements make
the latent components identifiable.
Our goal is to formalize that limitation and turn it into an experiment-design rule. The model is intentionally
small: it is not a molecular simulation, an epigenetic-clock implementation, or a model of human aging. It
separates a latent functional burden from a measurement-sensitive coordinate and asks which observations
are sufficient to distinguish their changes. We then construct a synthetic benchmark in which the true data-
generating process is known, allowing us to test identifiability, bias, uncertainty, and a constrained active-design
policy.

Related work and positioning
Epigenetic clocks are supervised predictors trained on methylation patterns, and their outputs can be useful
even when the measured CpGs are not causal drivers of aging. Horvath’s clock demonstrated cross-tissue age
prediction [Horvath, 2013]; PhenoAge and GrimAge incorporated phenotypic or mortality-associated information
to target outcomes beyond chronological age [Levine et al., 2018, Lu et al., 2019]. Large-scale language-model
prediction of biological age from health examination reports shows that prediction can also arise from clinical
text and population-level covariation [Li et al., 2025]. These approaches answer prediction questions; none
implies that directly changing a predictor’s inputs changes the underlying state that made the prediction useful.
The closest intervention evidence for our premise is the 2025 methylation-editing study by Liesenfelder
et al. It reports bystander methylation changes at many sites and measurable clock perturbations after
targeted editing [Liesenfelder et al., 2025]. This motivates a direct-marker path in the observation model.
The 2026 Transcriptional Rejuvenation Discovery Platform combines computational predictions, expression
comparisons, and perturbation screening to find candidate transcription factors associated with rejuvenation
without dedifferentiation [Sengstack et al., 2026]. We treat that as evidence that interventions and safety/identity
endpoints must be jointly considered, not as validation of our model.
Genotype-related clock variation is another relevant concern. The GIA preprint maps clock CpGs to blood
methylation QTLs and reports enrichment relative to matched unused probes [Lim, 2026]. Because it is a
preprint, its numerical findings are treated as preliminary and are not generalized beyond the studied data.
EnsembleAge’s multi-clock strategy illustrates a different mitigation: aggregate several predictors rather than
relying on one score [Haghani et al., 2025]. Multiple clocks can improve robustness, but correlated clocks may
share the same direct perturbation pathway and therefore need not identify functional repair.
Table 1 summarizes the closest lines of prior work and the inference boundary retained here. Our narrow
contribution is not the claim that clock scores can be misleading, that methylation is genetically influenced, or
that experimental design benefits from information criteria. Each has prior foundations. We combine these
ideas in an explicit causal state-space benchmark that (i) proves a simple observational-equivalence result, (ii)
specifies a rank-based condition for distinguishing repair and direct marker effects, and (iii) turns that condition
into a constrained sequential design algorithm with executable synthetic tests. The literature search is focused
rather than exhaustive; no absolute priority claim is made.

State variables and assumptions
Let a denote a latent functional-aging burden, m a molecular coordinate that directly influences the clock, g an
inherited set point, and i a cell-identity or safety coordinate. The model is discrete-time and dimensionless.
The coordinates are not measured biological quantities, and no parameter is calibrated to a living organism.
The model exists to test the logical relationship between interventions and observations.
Two intervention components are distinguished. u denotes a hypothetical repair-oriented perturbation that
changes a, and q denotes a marker-targeting perturbation that changes m. These labels describe their role
in the synthetic data-generating process; they are not prescriptions for experimental manipulation. A third
quantity, r, represents an identity-risk burden. We use linear local responses because identifiability can be
studied without assuming a complete nonlinear molecular mechanism.

Causal structure and measurement channels
The directed graph in fig. 2 separates three paths to the observations. A repair-oriented perturbation can change
the latent functional state, which can affect both the clock and the functional endpoint. A marker-targeting
perturbation can directly change the molecular coordinate used by the clock. Inherited variation can shift
baseline clock values. Identity drift is represented separately because a treatment that lowers an age-associated
score while damaging lineage fidelity is not necessarily a desirable rejuvenation intervention.
The graph is deliberately conservative. It does not assume that a particular methylation site is a causal aging
driver, that functional repair is one-dimensional, or that identity can be summarized by a single marker. The
purpose of the graph is to expose the assumptions required by the reduced equations. In particular, the absence
of a direct q →F arrow is a special case with ρ = 0; the benchmark also evaluates violations of that restriction.
Within-subject differences remove an additive, time-invariant βg term from the clock contrast. They do not
remove genotype-dependent treatment response, age-dependent cell composition, measurement drift, regression
to the mean, or any time-varying genetic effect. These alternatives are represented as failure modes rather than
assumed away. A robust experiment therefore needs both baseline stratification and randomized contrasts, with
independent functional and identity readouts collected on the same schedule.

An observational-equivalence result
We first state the main identifiability limitation.
Proposition 1 — scalar-clock non-identifiability
Let y = κu + αq, with known fixed inputs u, q and unknown coefficients κ, α. If u ̸= 0 and q ̸= 0, one
observed scalar y does not uniquely identify the pair (κ, α). For every scalar t, the transformed pair
κ′ = κ + tq,
α′ = α −tu
produces the same y.
The proposition is elementary but important. A good fit of a scalar endpoint does not establish which latent
path generated it. When u = q = 1, for example, (κ, α) = (0.25, 0.45) and (0.40, 0.30) both predict y = 0.70.
The clock contrast cannot distinguish a smaller repair effect with a larger direct marker effect from a larger
repair effect with a smaller marker effect. This is structural non-identifiability, not merely high variance. More
subjects measured under the same design reduce sampling noise but do not remove the equivalence.
If α were known from an independent calibration, κ could be recovered from the scalar contrast under the
model. If the design varies u and q independently across arms, the coefficients in the reduced linear equation
may become identifiable as regression parameters. But that alone does not demonstrate that the coefficient
associated with a repair-labeled input corresponds to a genuine functional improvement; the label is part of the
design hypothesis. An independent functional endpoint provides a separate test of the proposed mechanism.
Proposition 2 — rank condition for the local contrast model
For the observation rows
xC = (u, q, 0),
xF = (u, 0, −q)
associated with parameters θ = (κ, α, ρ), local linear identification requires the stacked design matrix
X to have column rank three. A single fixed intervention pair produces at most two distinct rows and
cannot identify all three coefficients. Independent variation of u and q, with both C and F observed,
can provide full rank.
The rank condition is necessary for the specified linear model, not sufficient for biological validity. Full rank
can coexist with biased measurement, incorrect causal structure, unmeasured confounding, or an endpoint that
does not track the function of interest. In a real experiment, one should report the smallest singular value and
condition number of the design matrix, not only a binary rank decision. Near-collinearity is practically similar
to non-identifiability because small measurement errors can create large coefficient uncertainty.

A constrained sequential design planner
The AI component in this paper is an uncertainty-aware design planner, not a generative model that invents
molecular mechanisms. At each step it evaluates a finite library of candidate perturbation/readout combinations,
estimates their incremental information gain, rejects candidates that violate the declared cost or risk budget,
and selects the admissible candidate with the highest expected gain. In a biological application, the candidate
library and risk scores would need to be supplied and reviewed by domain experts; the present code only uses
synthetic candidate arms.
The planner is intentionally transparent. It computes the expected information matrix from the local
Jacobian, scores candidate additions by the change in log-determinant, and logs the rejected candidates and
constraints. A more elaborate Bayesian planner could integrate posterior uncertainty over θ, model uncertainty,
and non-Gaussian outcomes. We do not claim that the greedy finite-library algorithm is globally optimal
for arbitrary designs. The benefit here is traceability: every choice can be reconstructed from a score and a
declared constraint.
The active planner differs from random or fixed allocation in one specific way. It seeks designs that separate
the repair coefficient from the marker coefficient, rather than simply maximizing the expected movement of
the clock. Under the synthetic model, a design that repeatedly samples the same mixture of u and q has a
poorly conditioned information matrix. A factorial-like allocation with independent variation can improve
parameter separation. This does not guarantee that the selected inputs correspond to safe or efficacious
biological interventions; it provides a way to choose informative tests once such inputs have been independently
justified.

Synthetic benchmark and computational protocol
The benchmark uses a fixed random seed and a linear local model with known coefficients. Each synthetic
subject has a baseline functional coordinate, a marker coordinate, and a binary inherited-setpoint variable. Clock
and functional observations are measured before and after a randomized intervention. The direct marker effect
changes the clock coordinate even when the true functional effect is zero. Noise is added to both measurement
channels. The script uses repeated Monte Carlo trials to estimate the distributions of clock and functional
contrasts, and it records all parameters and outputs in the release’s results directory.
The primary counterexample is the marker-only arm (u, q) = (0, q0). Under the data-generating equations,
its expected clock improvement is αq0, whereas the expected functional improvement is −ρq0. If ρ is near zero,
the clock may improve substantially without functional change. The repair-oriented arm (u, q) = (u0, 0) has
expected clock and functional improvement κu0 under the simplified model. The mixed arm combines both
effects and illustrates why a large clock movement can overstate the functional component.
We compare four analysis rules: (i) clock-only attribution, which treats all clock change as repair; (ii) a
paired functional contrast; (iii) a joint model using both channels and independently varied inputs; and (iv)
a constrained active-design policy. The benchmark measures bias, root mean squared error, threshold-based
false attribution, design-matrix rank, and approximate interval coverage. All metrics are generated from
the simulation; no clinical thresholds or biological units are implied. The false-attribution threshold is a
demonstration parameter, not a proposed decision threshold for real experiments.

Results: the measurement channel can mimic repair
In the marker-only scenario, the code estimates a mean clock improvement of 0.300 and a mean functional
improvement of -0.009. The difference arises by construction: the intervention has a direct path to the
clock-sensitive coordinate but no intended repair component. The estimated fraction of trials exceeding the
prespecified clock-only attribution threshold is 1.000. These outputs illustrate a failure mode of the attribution
rule; they do not estimate how often real rejuvenation studies make this error.

any sufficiently large reduction as repair. The functional contrast tests the latent-state component under the
model. When the functional endpoint is measured with noise, its estimate is less precise at small sample sizes;

Precision, uncertainty, and interval coverage
A design may be identifiable yet imprecise. The benchmark therefore varies sample size and computes Monte
Carlo RMSE for clock-only and functional estimators. The clock-only estimator can have a persistent bias
because its estimand is a mixture of functional repair and direct marker displacement. Its RMSE need not
vanish with sample size. By contrast, an independent functional endpoint has lower structural bias in the base
scenario, although its variance remains governed by noise and sample size.
Coverage is evaluated under both the base model and a misspecified model in which the marker-targeting
input also affects the functional endpoint. When the analyst assumes ρ = 0 despite a nonzero direct effect,
nominal intervals can miss the true κ more often than intended. The lesson is not that a particular endpoint is
universally trustworthy; rather, each endpoint requires a measurement model, negative controls, and a test of
plausible direct effects.

Repeated measures, batch effects, and latent composition
Paired measurements are attractive because they remove stable subject-specific baselines. Yet a within-subject
difference is not automatically causal. Time-varying confounding, regression to the mean, batch shifts, differential
cell survival, and changes in cell-type composition can still alter the observed contrast. The distinction matters
especially when the outcome is a molecular score constructed from features that may themselves respond
directly to the intervention.
A simple hierarchical extension is
Yijt = µ + βuuit + βqqit + bi + cj + δt + ϵijt,
where bi is a subject-level random intercept, cj is a batch effect, and δt represents a time-specific influence shared
across samples. This expression is a bookkeeping model, not a universal biological law. If each intervention is
confounded with a unique batch, the corresponding effects cannot be separated by fitting a more elaborate
mixed model alone. The design must contain overlap: multiple arms represented within batches, randomized
processing order where feasible, and technical controls that reveal assay drift.
Cell composition creates a second layer of ambiguity. Let the measured bulk clock be a weighted aggregate of
cell-state-specific values, Cbulk = P
k πkCk, where πk is the proportion of state k. A change in Cbulk can arise
from within-state molecular change, a shift in the πk, or both. These possibilities have different interpretations.
A treatment that selectively removes a high-clock subpopulation may lower the bulk score without resetting the
clock in surviving cells. Conversely, a beneficial within-cell change can be obscured by a shift in composition.
Bulk measurements alone do not generally distinguish these paths.
The decomposition is not solved by adding a composition covariate after the fact if the intervention itself
changes composition and composition lies on a causal pathway of interest. Adjusting for a post-intervention
variable can change the estimand or introduce collider bias under some causal structures. Investigators should
specify whether they seek a total intervention effect, a within-cell effect, a composition-standardized contrast, or
a decomposition into both components. These are different questions and may require different measurements
or assumptions.
A robust protocol should therefore combine paired baselines with randomized intervention assignment,
balanced batches, explicit composition measurements, and independent functional outcomes. When single-cell
assays are appropriate and feasible, cell-resolved data can help distinguish within-state changes from shifts
in population proportions; however, single-cell data introduce their own sampling, dropout, dissociation, and
annotation uncertainties. More granular data are not automatically more informative if the measurement
process is poorly calibrated or the relevant cell states are not identifiable.
Negative controls are especially valuable when they target a plausible measurement path without being
expected to repair the function of interest. A marker-sensitive control tests whether the clock can move in
the absence of the proposed repair pathway. A sham or vehicle control estimates temporal drift and handling
effects. An orthogonal functional assay tests whether the response generalizes beyond the original measurement
channel. The controls must be chosen for the actual system; the names of the arms do not guarantee that their
causal effects match the intended assumptions.

protocol should prespecify the candidate library, update rule, stopping criterion, and final inferential procedure.

Greedy design is computationally convenient, but it need not achieve the globally best set when candidates
interact or when constraints are non-submodular. The release therefore treats the selected arm set as a proposal
with a traceable score, not as a proof of global optimality. A future implementation could compare greedy
selection with exhaustive search on small candidate sets, mixed-integer optimization, or posterior-simulation
methods. Such comparisons would test the planner itself rather than assuming that a plausible-looking output

Sample-size logic and preregistered estimands
Sample size should be tied to the estimand and the weakest design direction, not to the desire for a narrow
interval around a convenient clock score. In a correctly specified linear model with independent homoscedastic
errors, information grows approximately linearly with replicated observations, so standard errors often decline on
the order of n−1/2. This familiar scaling does not resolve structural non-identifiability, and it can be misleading
when errors are correlated, measurement variance depends on the state, or the design is selected adaptively.
A useful planning exercise begins with a pilot-informed range for measurement variance and direct-effect
sensitivity. For each candidate sample size, simulate the full planned design, including missingness, batch
allocation, baseline correlation, and any sequential decision rule. Record bias, root-mean-square error, interval
coverage, false attribution of clock-only movement to repair, and the probability that a safety or identity
constraint is triggered. The target should be a decision-relevant operating characteristic—for example, the
probability that the identification region excludes a prespecified null range—rather than a single nominal power
number disconnected from model uncertainty.
The synthetic simulations in this paper are not a biological power calculation. Their parameters are chosen
to expose a mathematical failure mode and to verify the software path. They do not estimate the variance of
a real methylation assay, the prevalence of cell-state transitions, or the distribution of intervention effects in
humans or model organisms. Any real sample-size recommendation would require pilot data, a specific assay, a
declared population, and a defensible model for missingness and heterogeneity.
Preregistration should name the primary clock, independent functional endpoint, identity and safety outcomes,
contrast of interest, nuisance-effect bounds, exclusions, missing-data handling, and stopping rule. It should also
state which outcomes are confirmatory and which are exploratory. If multiple clocks or functional endpoints
are measured, multiplicity and endpoint selection must be addressed. Selecting the clock that improves most
after observing the data is not equivalent to testing a prespecified biological hypothesis.
The report should preserve negative results and discordant outcomes. If the clock improves but function
does not, the result is not a failed rejuvenation experiment that can be rescued by re-labeling the clock; it is
evidence against the specific claim that the observed clock movement establishes functional repair. If function

Stress tests and the boundary of interpretation
A mathematical model is most useful when it identifies where it can fail. The linear contrast model assumes
that the effects of u and q are additive over the tested range. Saturation, thresholds, dose interactions, delayed
effects, and state-dependent sensitivity can violate that assumption. A nonlinear model may be appropriate,
but adding nonlinear terms can also consume the independent variation required to identify them. The correct
response is not to fit the most flexible model automatically; it is to ask whether the design contains enough
information to distinguish the extra mechanism.
A first stress test is an interaction model, dC = κu + αq + γuq + ϵC. If all observations use the same ratio of
u to q, the interaction may be nearly collinear with the main effects. A second stress test allows the direct
marker effect to vary across subjects or batches, αi = α + ηi. A third introduces delayed response, replacing
ut by a distributed-lag term PL
ℓ=0 hℓut−ℓ. Each extension changes the design requirements. The parameters
cannot be declared identifiable merely because an optimizer returns finite estimates.
Measurement error in the intervention itself is another important threat. If the nominal dose differs from the
delivered exposure, regression coefficients can be attenuated or distorted. When dose measurement error is
correlated with batch or subject state, the bias may be more complicated than simple attenuation. Calibration
samples, randomized assignment, and independent exposure measures may be needed. A model that treats the
intervention input as known should explicitly state that assumption.
The causal graph can also be wrong. The proposed repair-labelled input may affect the clock through a direct
molecular path; the marker-targeting input may affect function; both inputs may change cell composition; and
an unmeasured stress response may affect all readouts. No rank condition eliminates these alternatives. They
require negative controls, independent readouts, intervention-specific knowledge, or a sensitivity analysis that
bounds the unmeasured paths. If no credible bound or control exists, the mechanism should remain unresolved.
These stress tests motivate a tiered interpretation. A result can establish a mathematical identity in the
synthetic model; demonstrate that the software recovers a known synthetic parameter under stated assumptions;
show robustness to a declared range of nuisance effects; or support a causal biological claim in a properly
designed experiment. These are four different evidentiary levels. The current release reaches only the first three
within a synthetic setting, and it does not claim the fourth.
The most decisive falsification is not a small change in one metric. It is a pattern that contradicts the central
measurement claim: a clock-only perturbation produces the expected clock response but no corresponding
independent functional response; the inferred repair coefficient changes sign under modest, independently
plausible direct-effect bounds; or the proposed model fails to predict held-out functional outcomes. Each
outcome would require the mechanism claim to be narrowed, revised, or withheld. A preregistered negative
result can be more informative than an apparently large clock reset whose causal interpretation is not identified.

Counterexamples and failure modes
The model generates several counterexamples to a simplistic clock-only interpretation. First, a marker-only
intervention can change C while κ = 0. Second, a genuine repair intervention can be obscured if the direct marker
effect has the opposite sign. Third, a functional readout can itself be directly affected by a marker-targeting
intervention, violating the assumption that it is an independent endpoint. Fourth, a genotype-by-treatment
interaction can survive within-subject differencing. Fifth, a well-conditioned design can still estimate the wrong
parameters if the observation equations omit a relevant path.
These counterexamples are not edge cases added after the fact; they define the assumptions under which the
method is interpretable. Table 6 converts the central claims into preregisterable tests, while Table 8 summarizes

each proposed intervention, the experimenter should specify at least one marker-only negative control, one
functional endpoint independent of the clock-training features, an identity/safety panel, and a perturbation
dose or timing contrast that can reveal direct measurement effects.

A falsifiable experimental protocol
A future biological study could test the model only after selecting a specific cell system, intervention, and
independently justified functional endpoint. We do not prescribe molecular targets or treatment parameters.
The protocol below is a design template: preregister the causal question, identify the proposed repair and
marker-sensitive paths, define the estimand and safety limits, and choose a measurement schedule that permits
the two paths to be separated.
The design should include untreated or vehicle controls, a marker-targeting control expected to move the
clock without the proposed repair mechanism, and at least one perturbation expected to change function
through an independently supported pathway. Randomize intervention components where scientifically and
ethically appropriate. Measure the clock, a functional outcome, cell identity, and relevant adverse outcomes
at baseline and follow-up. If the system is heterogeneous, use stratification or cell-resolved measurements
and record composition changes. The analysis should report both the total clock response and the functional
response rather than converting one into the other.
The primary falsification criterion is straightforward: if a clock-lowering perturbation fails to improve the
preregistered functional endpoint, or if the effect disappears after accounting for direct marker changes, the
study does not support the claim that the clock reduction indicates functional repair. A second criterion is
failure of the model’s predicted dose-response or interaction structure. A third is a meaningful increase in
identity loss or adverse outcomes, even if the clock improves. Null or adverse results are informative and must
be retained.

An explicit decision rule
The output of the planner is not a single rejuvenation score. It is a recommended set of admissible arms, their
predicted information contribution, the rank and conditioning of the resulting design, and a list of unresolved
assumptions. For a proposed biological application, the decision rule should be lexicographic: first satisfy
prespecified safety and identity constraints; next require the design to identify the target estimand under the
declared model; then maximize information per unit cost. A design that violates a hard safety constraint is not
rescued by a high information score.
The synthetic implementation uses a finite candidate library and greedy addition. At each step it evaluates
the log-determinant gain from each admissible candidate, subtracts a cost penalty, rejects candidates above
the risk ceiling, and chooses the best remaining candidate. The algorithm is auditable and deterministic for a
fixed seed. It is not a substitute for domain expertise, and its safety score is only as valid as the assumptions
supplied to it.
The design objective can be extended to incorporate prior uncertainty over parameters, expected model
discrimination, missingness, batch effects, and multiple tissue endpoints. Such extensions are worthwhile only if
the additional structure is estimable and validated. A complex planner can be confidently wrong when its prior
or risk model is misspecified. For this reason, we archive candidate scores, constraints, intermediate information
matrices, and the selected design rather than reporting only the final choice.

Ethical and translational boundaries
Aging interventions and cellular reprogramming have potential relevance to healthy lifespan, regenerative
medicine, and treatment of age-associated disease, but the gap between a synthetic design model and a
biological intervention is substantial. A false claim of rejuvenation could misdirect resources, obscure adverse
effects, or encourage premature translation. This paper therefore makes no clinical recommendation, no claim
of lifespan extension, and no assertion that a clock reset is inherently beneficial or harmful.
A valid application would require domain-specific safety review, suitable cell or organism models, preregistered
outcomes, appropriate controls, and ethical/regulatory oversight. For cellular reprogramming, preservation of
identity and avoidance of tumorigenic or dysplastic states are separate concerns from molecular age markers. A
lower score should never be treated as a safety guarantee. The proposed active-design planner should prioritize
the safest admissible experiments rather than maximize a surrogate endpoint without constraints.
The AI component also has limits. The planner does not infer causal biology from unverified data, and it does
not independently assign a molecular target to an intervention. Its role is to expose which experiment would
be informative under an explicit model. Human investigators must decide whether the model and candidate
interventions are scientifically plausible, ethically acceptable, and adequately controlled.

Discussion
The main result is a measurement-identifiability statement, not a new theory of aging. If a clock can respond to
direct marker perturbation, a clock-only outcome cannot generally identify functional repair. This is consistent
with the broader distinction between prediction and causal intervention. The recent literature gives concrete
reasons to model direct methylation effects and genetic regulation explicitly [Liesenfelder et al., 2025, Lim,
2026]. It also illustrates that intervention discovery can be combined with identity constraints rather than using
an age score as the sole objective [Sengstack et al., 2026].
The proposed response is methodological. Treat the clock as one measurement channel among several;
separate within-subject change from baseline between-person differences; randomize intervention components
where possible; include an independent functional endpoint and an identity/safety panel; and report rank,
conditioning, uncertainty, and counterexamples. If an experiment cannot distinguish the proposed paths, the
correct conclusion is that the mechanism remains unidentified, not that more subjects will necessarily resolve it.

There are limits to the linear model. Biological systems can exhibit nonlinear response, delayed adaptation,
feedback, threshold effects, cell-state mixtures, and intervention-specific changes in the measurement process. A
scalar latent functional coordinate is itself a simplification. In future work, the model could be extended to
a partially observed dynamical system with time-varying coefficients and multiple endpoints. But additional
complexity should be earned by data and identifiability analysis. A high-dimensional model can conceal rather
than solve the problem if its parameters cannot be distinguished by the experiment.

Limitations
First, the benchmark is synthetic and uses known linear equations. It demonstrates an identifiability failure that
follows from those equations, not the frequency or magnitude of the failure in real rejuvenation experiments.
Second, the functional endpoint is assumed to have a known interpretation and a simple direct-effect term.
Real functional measures may be noisy, tissue-specific, and causally affected by multiple intervention paths.
Third, the genotype model is additive and time-invariant; interactions, ancestry structure, and cell composition
require richer models and better data.
Fourth, the active-design method optimizes a finite candidate set using a local Fisher-information approxima-
tion and a stylized risk penalty. It does not guarantee global optimality for nonlinear models, and it cannot
make unsafe candidate interventions safe. Fifth, we did not analyze a public methylation dataset or reproduce
any published experiment. The literature review was focused, not exhaustive, and the topic-selection scores are
internal triage judgments rather than validated metrics of novelty or impact. Finally, no claim is made that the
proposed design rule has been implemented or prospectively tested in a biological laboratory.
27
Conclusion
A change in an epigenetic clock is not, by itself, an identified change in functional aging. In a minimal
state-space model, a scalar clock contrast can be generated by multiple combinations of latent repair and direct
marker displacement. This is a structural ambiguity, not merely a small-sample problem. Paired contrasts can
remove an additive inherited set point under stated assumptions, while independent perturbation variation and
functional measurements can improve identifiability.
The practical output is a falsifiable design rule: plan experiments to separate the repair path from the
measurement path, report the rank and conditioning of the design, and keep identity and safety as independent
outcomes. The synthetic benchmark demonstrates how a clock-only attribution rule can fail and provides
code to reproduce the result. Whether this framework improves real biological experiments remains an open
empirical question.

A
Proof of Proposition 1
For fixed nonzero u, q, the expected scalar clock contrast is y = κu + αq. For any real t, define κ′ = κ + tq and
α′ = α −tu. Then
κ′u + α′q = (κ + tq)u + (α −tu)q = κu + αq + t(qu −uq) = κu + αq.
Thus a continuum of parameter pairs yields the same expected observation. The pair is not uniquely identified
by y. The argument concerns the expectation; adding zero-mean noise changes uncertainty but does not remove
the structural equivalence.

Proof sketch for Proposition 2

full column rank. If all arms use a single fixed pair (u, q), the stacked matrix contains at most two distinct row

C
Active-design algorithm
1. Define a finite set of admissible input pairs (u, q), measurement channels, costs, and a prespecified identity-risk
score.
2. For each candidate, construct the observation Jacobian and the corresponding Fisher-information contribution
under the stated noise covariance.
3. At each sequential step, compute the increase in regularized log-determinant for every candidate that remains
within the cost and risk budgets.
4. Select the candidate with the largest admissible information gain; record its score, constraints, and rejected
alternatives.
5. After the planned allocation is selected, report the unregularized design rank, singular values, condition
number, and sensitivity to the risk model.
The released implementation uses a deterministic finite candidate library. It is a transparent greedy heuristic,
not a claim of global optimality.

Synthetic parameter register
The benchmark uses a fixed seed, dimensionless coefficients, Gaussian measurement noise, and repeated
simulated subjects. The complete parameter values and dependency versions are written by the script to
results/summary.json and results/environment_report.json. All figures are regenerated from the same
model code. No empirical dataset is downloaded or required.
